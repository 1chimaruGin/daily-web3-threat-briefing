# Web3 Threat Briefing — 2026-09-19

*Reconstructed on 2026-09-21 from archived sources; written after the fact, not same-day.*

> **Scope:** Multi-chain (EVM/Solidity · Solana/Rust · Cosmos/Move · cross-chain bridges). Priority: DeFi, wallets, key infrastructure.
> **Window:** Last ~24–48h (September 18–19 UTC). **Headline: after a genuinely quiet September 18, the 19th produced three separate incidents. MultiversX stopped producing blocks after an attacker exploited an atomicity flaw at the virtual-machine layer and wrote invalid state to mainnet — no loss figure has been published. Blink Wallet suspended all Lightning services mid-morning UTC after an attacker drained a few dozen custodial accounts, patched, and was back online ~14 hours later. And SlowMist's CISO warned that a weaponized Safari-to-kernel iOS exploit chain is being used to lift private keys and seed phrases straight out of the Keychain — the single most consequential disclosure of the day for anyone holding a mobile hot wallet.**

## 1. 🔴 Active exploits (last 24h)

### MultiversX — VM-Level Atomicity Flaw, Mainnet Halted (Sept 19)

**Chain:** MultiversX mainnet (WASM VM, not EVM)
**Status:** NETWORK HALTED — block production stopped. Fix built and under validation on a shadow fork; no restart time published as of end of Sept 19 UTC.
**Impact:** **No confirmed loss figure has been disclosed.** Do not attach a dollar number to this incident; none of the sources below publish one.

**Timeline (as reported):**

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| Sept 19, ~10:16 (UTC+8 ≈ 02:16 UTC) | MultiversX reports "anomalies" on mainnet and opens an emergency investigation, saying it will update within 12 hours |
| Sept 19–20 (announcement timestamped Sept 20 01:06 UTC+8 ≈ Sept 19 17:06 UTC) | Team confirms an attacker "attempted to exploit virtual machine-level atomicity issues, causing invalid state changes"; block production paused |

**Root cause (as described by the team and reporting):** a transaction-atomicity failure in the VM layer. Per the description relayed by reporting: "One operation fails, but an earlier state change remains. The network can then record a partial result that should not have existed by itself." In other words, a multi-step execution could be made to abort partway while earlier writes stayed committed — breaking the all-or-nothing guarantee that every downstream contract assumes.

**Why the halt rather than a patch-in-place:** stopping block production does two things at once — it prevents honest transactions from building on top of state that may already be wrong, and it denies the attacker a second attempt with the same method while engineers work out which balances and storage entries are actually invalid. The team said it is evaluating a *targeted* recovery that preserves finalized history and legitimate user state while reverting only the invalid changes from this incident.

**Operational guidance issued:** users told not to submit or rebroadcast transactions; exchanges and bridges told to stop EGLD and ESDT deposits and withdrawals. On Sept 20 the status page showed public API, xPortal, Explorer, Wallet, Bridge, xExchange and xLaunchpad as degraded, with gateway and index operational — service labels, not confirmation that transaction processing had resumed.

**Bug class:** logic-error (execution atomicity / partial state commit at the VM layer)

### Blink Wallet — Custodial Lightning Account Compromise (Sept 19)

**Chain:** Bitcoin / Lightning Network
**Status:** RESOLVED SAME DAY — services paused ~06:43 UTC, patch deployed, services restored ~20:19 UTC (4:19 p.m. ET). Full post-mortem promised, not published as of Sept 19.
**Impact:** A "few dozen" custodial accounts drained. **No USD or BTC loss figure has been disclosed.**

**What happened:** Blink paused all services to investigate after an attacker gained access to a limited number of its custodial accounts and withdrew funds. The company's statement in full:

> "We've paused Blink services while we investigate a security incident. An attacker accessed a limited number of custodial accounts and withdrew funds. The large majority of funds are secure. Non-custodial wallets are not affected."

**What is and is not known:**
- **Confirmed:** custodial accounts only — wallets where Blink holds keys on the user's behalf. Non-custodial users unaffected. A patch was deployed and verified before services came back. Blink committed that "every affected account is identified and will be made whole."
- **Not disclosed:** the attack vector. Blink published no technical detail on how the attacker obtained access on the 19th.
- **Unconfirmed:** reporting noted it had not been established whether Blink's Spark Bitcoin payment protocol was the source of the incident. Treat any Spark attribution as unconfirmed.

**Why it belongs in a smart-contract briefing:** it does not involve a contract bug, and that is the point. This is a backend/account-control failure in a product users treat as a Bitcoin wallet. The relevant lesson is the custodial/non-custodial split inside a single consumer wallet brand — the same brand name, the same app, two completely different trust models, and only one of them was exposed.

**Bug class:** access-control / key-management (server-side custodial account compromise; mechanism undisclosed)

### Fetch.ai + NuNet — Leaked Conversion-Authorizer Key, ~$2.01M (Sept 19)

> **On the date.** The drain is a **Sept 19** event. Blockaid and PeckShield both place the attack on September 19, and Coinpedia's write-up carrying that attribution was itself published at **03:12 UTC on Sept 20** — only three hours into the 20th, which rules out a Sept 20 UTC drain. The Sept 20 dates attached to this incident elsewhere (CoinDesk/Yahoo, The Cryptonomist) are **reporting** dates: Blockaid's public report and Fetch.ai's own preliminary on-chain analysis both landed on the 20th. None of the sources reviewed publishes the block timestamp, so if you are reconciling against an on-chain timeline, pull the block yourself.

**Chain:** Ethereum
**Status:** Confirmed by Blockaid and PeckShield. Fetch.ai published a preliminary on-chain analysis.
**Impact:** ~$2.01M total across the attacker's wallet cluster — approximately 8.7M FET (~$1.53–1.56M) from Fetch.ai, plus 408.5M NTX minted at NuNet (~$452,000–463,000; figures vary slightly by source). Proceeds converted to roughly 546 ETH.

**Root cause:** `TokenConversionManagerV3.conversionIn()` accepted a **single externally-owned-account ECDSA signature as its sole authorization check**, and — unlike the outbound path — **lacked the `checkLimits(amount)` modifier present in `conversionOut()`**. The attacker held a valid conversion-authorizer signature and used one call to release the converter's entire remaining FET inventory. Fetch.ai's preliminary analysis traces the attack path to a **leaked signing key**, not a logic bug in the signature verification itself.

The NuNet leg is a separate primitive with the same underlying failure: 408.5M NTX were minted directly through NuNet's **deployer account**. Same operator, linked by shared destination-wallet clusters.

**The pattern worth internalizing:** the inbound and outbound halves of the same bridge/converter had **asymmetric safety rails**. Someone remembered to cap `conversionOut` and did not cap `conversionIn`. A leaked key that should have cost the protocol a rate-limited amount instead cost it the entire inventory in one transaction.

**Market impact:** NTX fell to an all-time low (reported variously as −65% and −90%+; the token's absolute size makes the percentage noisy). FET fell around 5%.

**Bug class:** key-management (leaked authorizer key) compounded by access-control (missing limit modifier on the inbound path)

### DCENT App Wallet — Signing Vulnerability / Mass Key Compromise (Day 5)

**Chains:** XRP Ledger (confirmed drains); Bitcoin, Ethereum, TRON and EVM chains flagged as in-scope by the vendor
**Status:** ACTIVE / UNRESOLVED. DCENT's *Preliminary Incident Report* (dated Sept 17 KST) **deliberately withholds the technical root cause**, on the stated grounds that disclosure "could be used in identical or similar attacks or lead to further harm."
**Impact known as of Sept 19:** ~2,009,321 XRP (~$2.8M at the time) drained from 1,552 wallets in a 2h05m window, 16:29–18:34 UTC on **Sept 15** — the date the Day count runs from, and the date SlowMist's archive carries, at a $6.57M multi-chain figure.

> **Do not backdate the revised number.** The larger figure — ~9.3M XRP across 6,160 addresses, with the v10.0.0 app update instruction — was published on **September 20** and was not the known figure on the 19th. Treat ~$2.8M / 1,552 wallets as the contemporaneous Sept 19 number and the ~9.3M XRP / 6,160-address revision as a later correction.

**Affected scope (per vendor):** DCENT **App Wallet** (the software wallet), **versions earlier than 8.1.0** (released 2025-11-05), Android and iOS. What matters is the version **at the time of signing**, not the version currently installed. Hardware-wallet-only users who never typed a seed into the app are unaffected.

**Why the vendor scoped it to "signing":** DCENT titled its own report *App Wallet Signing Vulnerability* and scoped exposure to addresses with signing history rather than to all addresses holding a seed. That shape is consistent with a flaw on the signing path — nonce generation or key derivation, i.e. keys recoverable from published signatures — rather than server-side seed exfiltration. **This is inference, not confirmation; DCENT has not said which.** Supporting evidence for pre-compromised keys: the XRP drain worked from a pre-compiled victim list ordered by account creation date rather than by balance, meaning the attackers were not scanning the ledger live.

**Remediation:** update from official stores only, generate a **new** recovery phrase, move all coins, tokens, NFTs and staked assets to the new address, never reuse the old one.

**Bug class:** key-management (signing-path flaw, mechanism undisclosed)

### Liquid Network (Blockstream) — $320M Range-Proof Cache Collision (Day 13)

**Chain:** Liquid (Bitcoin sidechain)
**Status:** PARTIAL RECOVERY. Block production and ordinary transactions resumed Sept 10; **peg-ins and peg-outs remain suspended** with no published restart date. **No new Liquid development was reported in the Sept 18–19 window** — state-of-play only.
**Outstanding:** 598.5 BTC (~$47M) never returned.

**Recap:** an attacker exploited a **range-proof verification cache-key collision** in Elements, the software powering Liquid. Per Chainalysis: "The hackers exploited this by first getting valid data verified and cached, then submitting different, invalid data that pointed to the same cached result." The cache key omitted security-relevant context, so a proof already verified in one context validated a fresh issuance — minting ~4,000 unbacked L-BTC, redeemed for real BTC. Federation reserves fell from ~4,205 BTC to 197 BTC. **No federation member private keys were compromised.** The attacker returned ~3,400 BTC after claiming white-hat status, then demanded 10% as a "bug bounty"; Blockstream publicly refused and referred the matter to law enforcement, warning that paying would set a dangerous precedent.

> **Date note:** sources differ on the exploit date — CoinDesk and most reporting place it on Sept 6, Chainalysis describes it as "the weekend of September 7–8." The Day count here continues the series convention used in the Sept 18 briefing (Day 12).

**Patch:** Elements v23.3.4.

**Bug class:** logic-error (verification-cache key omitting security-relevant context — validation-cache poisoning)

### SlowMist — Operational Full-Chain iOS Exploit Stealing Wallet Keys (Sept 19) 🔴

**Severity:** Treat as critical for any mobile hot wallet. **No CVE identifier has been assigned or published** in the sources below, and SlowMist did not name the underlying bugs.

**What was disclosed:** On September 19, SlowMist CISO **23pds** warned iPhone users to update immediately, stating that attackers have **operationalized** a full-chain iOS exploit capable of silently extracting private keys and mnemonic seed phrases from crypto wallets.

**The chain, step by step:**

| Stage | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| 1. Delivery | Target visits a malicious webpage in Safari — social engineering or watering-hole link |
| 2. Initial primitive | Memory corruption in **WebKit / JavaScriptCore** grants arbitrary read/write at the JavaScript layer |
| 3. Native execution | **Pointer Authentication Code (PAC) bypass** → native code execution |
| 4. Sandbox | Escape from the **WebContent** sandbox |
| 5. Privilege | **Kernel privilege escalation** to root |
| 6. Objective | Read the device **Keychain** and wallet app data — private keys, recovery phrases |

**Affected versions:** reported as **iOS 13 through iOS 26.5**, with the upper bound described as **still pending final confirmation**. Treat the ceiling as unconfirmed.

**Why this matters more than a typical phishing warning:** there is **no seed-phrase entry step**. The user does not get tricked into typing anything. Visiting a link is the whole interaction. Every mitigation that depends on user vigilance about entering their backup into a fake app — the defence that limits damage in ordinary seed-phishing campaigns — is irrelevant here. And self-custody users have no exchange-side recovery to fall back on.

**Mitigation (SlowMist's own guidance):**
1. Update iOS to the latest version immediately.
2. Avoid clicking unsolicited links in Safari.
3. If you held a hot wallet on a vulnerable device: **generate new keys on a clean, updated device and move funds.** An OS update does not un-steal a key that has already been read.

**Bug class:** client-side-exploit-chain (browser memory corruption → PAC bypass → sandbox escape → kernel LPE → Keychain theft)

### CVE-2026-57583 — OpenZeppelin Contracts Wizard Comment-Injection (LOW — outside the window, listed for completeness)

**Published:** September 14, 2026 — **five days before this window.** It is listed here only because it is the most recent Web3 CVE and has not been superseded; it is not new on the 19th.

**Affected / patched:**

| Package | Affected | Patched |
|---|---|---|
| `@openzeppelin/wizard` | < 0.10.11 | 0.10.11 |
| `@openzeppelin/wizard-cairo` | < 3.0.1 | 3.0.1 |
| `@openzeppelin/wizard-stellar` | < 0.6.2 | 0.6.2 |
| `@openzeppelin/wizard-stylus` | < 0.3.1 | 0.3.1 |

**Severity:** CVSS **3.3 (Low)**.
**What it does:** the `setInfo` code path prints `info.securityContact` and `info.license` verbatim into single-line comments in generated Solidity, Cairo, Stellar/Soroban and Stylus source. A line terminator ends the comment early, so following input is emitted as source declarations.
**Realistic exposure:** generated-source integrity only. Exploitation requires an integration that populates those fields from **untrusted input** and a user who then consumes the generated source. Normal self-service web, AI-assistant, CLI and self-hosted API use does not cross that trust boundary — which is why this is a 3.3 and not the 8.8 that the earlier Wizard injection issue carried.

**Bug class:** code-injection (generated-source integrity)

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Signature-authorized entry points: diff the inbound path against the outbound path**

Fetch.ai is the cleanest teaching case of the week and the bug is trivially greppable. `conversionOut()` had `checkLimits(amount)`. `conversionIn()` did not. Both accepted a single EOA ECDSA signature as the sole authorization. One leaked key therefore cost the entire converter inventory instead of one rate-limited tranche.

For every bridge, converter, minter or wrapper in scope:
- List every function gated by a signature, a `signer` role, an `authorizer`, a `relayer` or an `operator`. For each, write down **exactly which modifiers it carries**.
- Then diff the matched pairs — `in`/`out`, `deposit`/`withdraw`, `lock`/`release`, `mint`/`burn`. **Any modifier present on one side and absent on the other is the finding.** Rate limits, per-tx caps, daily caps, pause checks, and nonce/replay guards all count.
- Count the signers. A single EOA signature authorizing an unbounded transfer of the contract's whole balance is a high-severity finding on its own, independently of whether the key is currently leaked — the key *will* leak eventually, and the only question is what it costs when it does.
- Check whether the token's mint authority is a deployer EOA (the NuNet leg). Deployer-key mint authority on a live token is a standing single point of failure.

**2. Execution atomicity on non-EVM VMs — hunt partial-state commits**

MultiversX halted an entire chain over this. The EVM's all-or-nothing revert semantics are so reliable that auditors stop thinking about them; on WASM VMs, Move, Solana's runtime and Cosmos custom modules, atomicity is a property the VM implementers had to *build*, and it can be broken.

- Find every place a target's execution model allows a sub-call, cross-shard message, async callback, or promise to fail **after** state has already been written by the same logical operation. Does the failure roll back the earlier write, or leave it?
- On sharded or async architectures specifically: an operation that spans shards is not one transaction. Ask what the state looks like between the two halves, and whether that intermediate state is observable or actionable by anyone else.
- In Cosmos SDK custom modules: do `BeginBlock`/`EndBlock` handlers trust state written by `MsgServer` handlers in the same block without re-validating?
- Look for gas exhaustion, storage-limit and depth-limit failures as the *trigger* — those are the failure modes most likely to land mid-operation rather than at entry.

**3. Aggregator-sourced price feeds — attack the pool-selection step, not the price**

Carried forward from Nostra, still the highest-yield lending-market hook in scope. The clever move was not the wash trade, it was **becoming the reference pool**. If a target reads prices from a data aggregator (GeckoTerminal, DEX Screener, CoinGecko on-chain endpoints) rather than a hardened oracle, the selection heuristic *is* the attack surface:

- What ranks a pool as canonical — recent volume, displayed liquidity, pair creation order? Each is cheap to fake.
- Can an attacker **create** a new pool for the asset against any quote token and have it picked up? Is there a minimum-depth floor, a minimum-age requirement, or an allowlist of pool addresses?
- Is there a deviation circuit breaker — a hard reject on an N% move between updates — or does the protocol consume whatever it is handed?
- TWAP or spot? A single-block spot read from an attacker-creatable pool is a total compromise of the lending market.
- Separately, tabulate `max_borrowable_against_asset` against each collateral asset's **free-float** market cap (not FDV). Nostra let a ~$550K float back a $3.5M borrow. The protocol's own governance token is almost always the thinnest and almost always listed.

**4. Treat every mobile hot wallet key on an un-updated iPhone as burned**

This is an operational hook, not a code hook, and it applies to you and to every client whose funds you are responsible for. Because the iOS chain requires no seed entry and reaches the Keychain, the correct posture is **key rotation, not just patching**:

- Any device that was on iOS ≤ 26.5 and browsed untrusted links while holding a hot wallet: update, then **generate fresh keys on a clean device** and migrate. Updating alone does nothing for a key already exfiltrated.
- Audit where your own operational keys live. Testnet deployer keys, bounty-payout addresses and multisig signer devices on mobile are all in scope.
- For protocols in scope for an audit: if a signer, guardian, relayer or oracle-updater role is held by a key that lives on a phone, that is worth writing up as an operational-security finding in the report, even though it is not a contract bug. Fetch.ai's incident began with a leaked signing key, not a logic flaw.
