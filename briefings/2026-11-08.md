## 1. 🔴 Active exploits (last 24h)

### Keplr Wallet / Web3Auth — Embedded-Wallet Private-Key Breach (Active, No Confirmed Loss Total)

**Chain:** Cosmos ecosystem directly via Keplr, the affected wallet; broader blast radius via Web3Auth, a cross-chain embedded-wallet/key-management (social-login) infrastructure provider used by multiple apps, including Dungeon Wallet. Web3Auth is owned by Consensys (MetaMask's parent); Web3Auth's initial investigation found no evidence MetaMask users were impacted, though this is a preliminary, not final, finding.
**Status:** Active. Google/Apple login remains paused at Keplr; Web3Auth's embedded wallet service remains paused; whitehat recovery sweeps ongoing as of the most recent update (Nov 7, ~17:17 UTC).
**Impact:** Neither Keplr nor Web3Auth has published a confirmed loss total, a count of affected accounts, or a technical root-cause write-up. Keplr co-founder Josh Lee gave an informal estimate that roughly **20% of social-login-enabled accounts** could be at risk, with only a fraction of that 20% actually exploited and the rest potentially recoverable. Unverified, non-vendor-confirmed tracking circulating in the community cites figures ranging from an initial estimate exceeding $700,000 up to several million dollars; none of these figures carry vendor or independent-firm confirmation and are not repeated here as fact. Accounts created with a recovery phrase or hardware wallet are confirmed **not** affected.
**What happened (all times UTC, Nov 6-7, 2026):**
- **21:13, Nov 6** — Web3Auth announces on X that it is aware of "a security issue affecting Web3Auth's services used by some third parties" and has temporarily paused its embedded wallet service; says its initial investigation found no evidence MetaMask users are impacted. Keplr is not named at this point.
- **04:38, Nov 7** — Keplr issues its first public warning: unauthorized transactions reported on some Google/Apple-login accounts; recovery-phrase/hardware-wallet accounts unaffected; users told not to send new funds to social-login accounts, not to re-import/recreate accounts elsewhere, and to treat any "recovery" DMs as phishing. Google/Apple login paused.
- **16:53, Nov 7** — Web3Auth update: identifies "a security issue in Web3Auth infrastructure affecting certain embedded wallets used by Keplr"; says it is working with Keplr to secure assets; warns against unofficial recovery offers.
- **17:06-17:07, Nov 7** — Web3Auth says whitehat recovery has begun on vulnerable-but-not-yet-drained wallets, "using the same path as the original attacker" to move funds to safety; some of these protective transfers were themselves mistaken for attacker activity by observers. Recovered funds are described as safe; a verified list of recovery addresses is being prepared so users can distinguish legitimate protective transfers from theft.
- **17:17, Nov 7** — Keplr reiterates guidance and separately clarifies that some accounts may show a reduced or empty balance purely because of the ongoing whitehat mitigation effort, not necessarily because of attacker action.
**Root cause:** A vulnerability in Web3Auth's infrastructure — described only as a "private-key breach" affecting embedded/social-login wallets — exposed private keys for some accounts created via Google or Apple login. Web3Auth's embedded-wallet model generates and holds keys for users via its own infrastructure rather than ever showing the user a seed phrase, which is why recovery-phrase/hardware-wallet accounts are unaffected. No CVE, technical write-up, or exploit mechanism has been published by either company as of writing.
**Remediation:** Google/Apple login paused at Keplr; Web3Auth's embedded wallet service paused; whitehat protective-transfer sweeps in progress; verified recovery-address list in preparation; users advised to move any remaining liquid assets in a social-login account to a recovery-phrase or hardware-wallet account and to ignore unsolicited recovery offers, trusting only official @keplrwallet and @Web3Auth channels.
**Attribution:** None disclosed. Referred to only as "the original attacker" in official communications; no on-chain attacker address or threat-actor name has been published by either company.
**Bug class:** Key-management / embedded-wallet custody-infrastructure compromise at a third-party vendor serving multiple downstream wallet apps — a different threat model than a smart-contract bug, since the vulnerability sits in the off-chain key-generation/storage layer rather than on-chain logic, and its blast radius is defined by which apps integrate the vendor's SDK rather than which chain they're on.

### Suspected Railway-Vulnerability Hot-Wallet Drain — 96 Wallets Emptied in ~70 Minutes (New, Unconfirmed Link)

**Chain:** Chain-agnostic hot-wallet compromise; attacker exited via Relay, an EVM-side bridging/transfer service.
**Status:** Attack window closed (~70 minutes on Nov 6); Relay has blocked the attacker's flagged addresses; full scope still being assessed by the affected parties.
**Impact:** No confirmed total loss. Carlos, described as Head of Growth at "Definitive," reported his own previously-used hot wallet was emptied and said the attacker routed at least **$110,000+ in stablecoins** through Relay's RelayDepository in two batch withdrawals; he had not yet completed loss analysis across other affected chains at time of reporting, so this is a partial figure, not a total.
**What happened:** On Nov 6, 2026, an attack traced to the address/handle **"noble.base.eth"** drained 96 hot wallets within approximately 70 minutes. Critically, the transfers were signed using the victims' own private keys — this points to leaked mnemonics or private keys rather than a smart-contract exploit. Multiple outlets (TokenPost, KuCoin/PANews) describe this as a **"suspected"** Railway-related attack, a label that is explicitly caveated as tentative in every source checked.
**Root cause:** Unconfirmed. Importantly, none of the sources reviewed for this briefing explicitly name or date the prior **October 21, 2026 Railway cloud-platform breach** (in which Railway disclosed unauthorized access to customer environment variables, including some stored cryptocurrency wallet private keys) as the confirmed source of these leaked keys — the "Railway" label here is treated by reporters as an ongoing/background suspicion attached to the attacker's own claims or wallet-origin pattern, not as a verified reuse of the October breach's leaked secrets. Readers should treat the connection between this Nov 6 drain and the standing Railway incident (tracked separately below, Day 18) as **unconfirmed**.
**Remediation:** Relay blocked the attacker's flagged addresses; affected users urged to rotate keys on any wallet whose credentials may ever have transited Railway's environment-variable storage.
**Attribution:** None beyond the attacker-controlled handle "noble.base.eth"; no threat-actor name or nation-state attribution offered.
**Bug class:** Leaked-credential/private-key reuse resulting in direct hot-wallet drains signed with victims' own keys — distinct from a smart-contract bug; relevant regardless of chain, since the compromise is at the key-custody layer.

## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

**Trust Wallet phishing clone (trastwallet.cam) — real and warned against; a separate $1.1M iOS-exploit claim remains unverified:** On Nov 5, 2026, security researcher Anurag (@Malwarehunterr) flagged a typosquat site, "trastwallet[.]cam" (note: .cam, not .com), that tricks visitors into connecting their wallets under the pretext of "reviewing transfers." The site's JavaScript supports Trust Wallet, Phantom and OKX Wallet, and can scan connected wallet balances via a backend API and prepare transfers to preset addresses; transaction signing was reported disabled at the time of the researcher's check, meaning no confirmed drains have been tied to this specific site. Binance co-founder Changpeng Zhao (CZ) amplified the warning on X: "More phishing websites out there… .cam is not .com." Separately and independently, an unverified claim circulating the same day via a single X account ("Emperor.SOL") alleges roughly **$1.1 million** was stolen from Trust Wallet users through an exploit on devices running iOS 18.5, said to extract seed phrases with no transaction signature required; a posted screenshot shows USDT transfers (500,000 / 100,000 / 190,000) toward Binance deposit addresses, but does not establish how those transactions connect to the alleged exploit. Trust Wallet support has reportedly urged users to update iOS; neither Trust Wallet nor any independent security firm has confirmed the $1.1M figure, the exploit's existence, or its mechanism as of writing. Treat the phishing site as a confirmed, live threat and the $1.1M iOS-exploit claim as unconfirmed.

**CryptoPayment Gateway WordPress plugin — CVE-2026-81648, CVSS 10.0, no patch:** Unchanged from prior editions. No known fix exists; plugin remains removed from the WordPress Plugin Directory. Mitigation: remove the plugin, block the endpoint at the WAF layer, rotate any stored wallet credentials.

**Solana validator Linux kernel flaw — CVE-2026-98432, CVSS 9.8, Ubuntu still unpatched:** Unchanged from prior editions. AWS has shipped patches for Amazon Linux 2/2023; Ubuntu 24.04/22.04/26.10/26.04 remain vulnerable. ## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Third-party embedded-wallet/MPC key-management vendors as a single point of failure across many apps (Keplr/Web3Auth, this window)**

The Keplr incident isn't a Keplr bug — it's a Web3Auth infrastructure bug that happens to manifest through every app that integrates Web3Auth's embedded-wallet SDK (Keplr, Dungeon Wallet, and potentially others not yet named). If you're auditing or bounty-hunting any wallet app that offers "login with Google/Apple," treat the embedded-wallet vendor's key-generation and storage infrastructure as in-scope, not out-of-scope just because it's "someone else's code." Ask specifically: how are keys generated (MPC/DKG vs. single-point custody), where are key shares stored, what's the blast radius if the vendor is compromised, and is there a documented incident-response path that doesn't require the vendor to publish a root-cause before users can act?

**2. Whitehat rescue transactions colliding with incident response, and the UX gap that creates (Keplr/Web3Auth, this window)**

Web3Auth's own protective transfers were mistaken for attacker activity by observers and apparently by some affected users — a reminder that a "rescue the funds first, explain later" response can itself look identical to theft from the outside, and can show up as sudden "reduced or empty balance" that erodes user trust even when done correctly. For incident-response planning: publish verified rescue-destination addresses and a clear before/after accounting trail as early as possible, even before a root cause is ready, to avoid the rescue-vs-theft confusion seen here.

**3. Leaked secrets from a past breach re-surfacing as a live threat weeks later (suspected Railway link, this window)**

Whether or not this Nov 6 drain is confirmed to reuse secrets from the Oct 21 Railway breach, the pattern is worth internalizing regardless: leaked environment-variable secrets (API keys, wallet private keys, deployment tokens) don't expire on their own, and attackers can sit on them for weeks before using them. If you were ever a Railway customer (or any cloud-platform customer that disclosed a secrets breach), rotating credentials once at disclosure time is not sufficient reassurance months later — verify rotation actually happened for every secret class, not just the ones easiest to rotate.

**4. Volumetric API/DDoS campaigns against DeFi protocol backends as a non-contract attack surface (Loopscale, this window)**

Loopscale's week-long freeze had nothing to do with a smart-contract bug — it was a sustained high-volume API attack against the protocol's off-chain infrastructure, severe enough to force a full protocol pause out of caution even though on-chain logic was never touched. For protocols with off-chain components (oracles, API-gated vault operations, bots), rate-limiting and DDoS resilience on those components is as load-bearing for user-fund safety as the Solidity/Rust code itself — audit the infrastructure, not just the contracts.

**5. Typosquat phishing sites as a persistent, low-effort wallet-drain vector worth distinguishing from exploit claims (Trust Wallet, this window)**

The trastwallet.cam site is a real, if currently signing-disabled, threat; the $1.1M iOS-exploit claim circulating alongside it is unverified and sourced to a single account. For bounty/research purposes, keep these two threat classes separate in your own tracking — a confirmed phishing clone and an unconfirmed device-exploit claim call for different responses (user warnings vs. technical investigation), and conflating them (as some social-media coverage did this window) makes both harder to act on correctly.
