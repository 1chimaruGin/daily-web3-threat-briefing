### Ledger-Linked Hardware Wallets — CryptoBilis Drain Grows to ~$102M as Donjon Lab Details Implant Mechanics (Day 10)

**Chain:** Bitcoin, Ethereum, TRON, Solana, BNB Chain, Polygon (multi-chain victim wallets).
**Status:** Active investigation, first disclosed Oct 9, 2026. ### Pirate Chain (ARRR) — Ironwood Turnstile Bug Mints 39.8M ARRR, Unilateral Rollback Plan Abandoned (Day 5)

**Chain:** Pirate Chain (ARRR).
**Status:** Active, first disclosed Oct 14, 2026. An attacker exploited a validation flaw in the transparent-layer "turnstile" accounting introduced by Pirate Chain's **Ironwood** upgrade — activated just 11 days earlier on Oct 3 — to mint approximately **39.8 million ARRR**, raising total supply by roughly 20%. The minted coins were immediately moved into the Sapling shielded pool. On Oct 15, the team announced a plan to roll the blockchain back to Oct 14, 1:00 PM PT (20:00 UTC), discarding subsequent blocks. That plan was **reversed on Oct 16**: the team stated its decentralized structure makes it "not possible to freeze the network or unilaterally rollback its blockchain history," and pivoted instead to a forthcoming protocol-upgrade/fix proposal plus voluntary cooperation — dPoW notaries paused notarizations, exchanges and mining pools were asked to voluntarily halt operations, and NonKYC exchange flagged and restricted suspicious deposit accounts. The network itself remains online; users are advised not to move ARRR. No outlet has published a confirmed USD loss figure; at the Oct 16 spot price (~$0.2596/ARRR per CryptoTimes), the minted tokens would be worth roughly $10M on paper, but they sit frozen/illiquid in a shielded pool and no formal loss estimate exists — treat that $10M figure as this briefing's own back-of-envelope estimate, not a reported number.
**What happened / root cause:** Two combined validation failures in the Ironwood turnstile's supply-accounting logic — a bypassed minting-limit/issuance check, plus an incorrect interpretation of output scripts. The flaw sits in the transparent accounting layer, not in the Sapling shielded cryptography itself; no evidence of compromised notary keys or broken zk-SNARK soundness.
**Bug class:** logic-error / validation-bypass (upgrade-introduced minting-limit check failure, compounded by output-script misparsing — both surfaced within 11 days of a mainnet consensus-level upgrade).

### UNCX Network — Vesting V2 Share-Accounting Bug Escalates to ~$1.49M Across 4 Chains as Copycats Pile On (Day 5)

**Chain:** Ethereum, BNB Chain, Base, Robinhood Chain.
**Status:** Active, first disclosed Oct 14, 2026 as an $86,000 Ethereum-only drain; escalated to a confirmed **~$1.49M across four chains** by Oct 15 as the bug became public and copycat wallets plus MEV bots exploited the same path: ~$969K on BNB Chain, ~$242K on Ethereum, ~$169K on Robinhood Chain, ~$114K on Base, across 11 drain transactions and 10 affected tokens (pepecoin, EDGEN, ZC, X, LO0P, PAAL on Ethereum; IMO on Base; BTCLE on BNB Chain; CASHCAT and RKST on Robinhood Chain). UNCX's Oct 15 response: froze new vesting positions, raised lock fees to 1,000 ETH per lock and token fees to the 5% maximum, and is coordinating with law enforcement and SEAL 911. As of UNCX's last statement, no funds had been returned and a full incident report/recovery plan was still pending. Liquidity lockers and legacy vesting products were not affected.
**What happened / root cause:** An accounting flaw in UNCX's shared **VestingManager** contract: the `release()` function failed to decrement the position's recorded share count when tokens were released, and the `cancel()` function then re-read that stale (un-decremented) value and subtracted the unvested portion a second time — inflating the apparent remaining share value. The attacker opened three vesting positions, sacrificed two via a release-then-cancel sequence to inflate the shared pool's accounting, then drained the third against the now-inflated balance.
**Bug class:** accounting / invariant-violation (double-count of released vs. canceled vesting shares against a shared pool's global share counter).

## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

**NEAR Intents publishes post-mortem on its Oct 1 $3.8M deposit-accounting exploit (new this window, Oct 16).** The incident itself predates this briefing's window (exploited Sept 29-Oct 1, fully recovered within 36 hours of detection), but the technical post-mortem, published Oct 16 by contributor Alex Shevchenko, is worth reading for its root cause: NEAR Intents' deposit feature called a third-party contract as part of processing a deposit; when that downstream call failed, the deposited tokens were correctly returned to the depositor, **but the internal deposit record was not reversed** — leaving a credited balance on the books with no funds backing it. The attacker deployed a receiver contract engineered to make the notification call fail, then repeated the pattern across 23 transactions between Sept 29 16:14 UTC and Oct 1 05:54 UTC, accumulating ~$3.8M in phantom balances and withdrawing them through Omni, NEAR Intents' cross-chain infrastructure. The team detected withdrawal anomalies at 08:04 UTC on Oct 1 and paused the Intents contract 29 minutes later; all funds were returned and users made whole within 36 hours. Remediation: the deposit-notification feature was removed entirely, unusual-balance monitoring was added, and an automatic pause mechanism was introduced. Separately, NEAR Intents paid a $300,000 bounty on Oct 7 for an unrelated vulnerability report in the same general area. For bug hunters: this is a clean, general-purpose lesson — any failure path that reverts an external transfer must be checked for whether it also reverts every *internal* ledger entry tied to that transfer, not just the token movement itself.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Share/position accounting that double-counts across a release-then-cancel sequence (UNCX pattern, new this window)**

UNCX's bug is a clean, generalizable template: a shared-pool contract tracked per-position share counts, but the function that released tokens didn't decrement the position's recorded share count, so a later cancellation re-read the stale value and subtracted again — net effect, the same shares got "returned" twice against the pool's global accounting. Any vesting, staking, or shared-liquidity contract with separate release/cancel/withdraw code paths touching a common counter is worth checking for exactly this: does every path that changes a position's share balance also update *every* other counter derived from it, including ones only touched by a different function?

**2. Failed-callback paths that revert the transfer but not the internal ledger entry (NEAR Intents pattern, new this window)**

The NEAR Intents bug is the mirror image of a classic reentrancy bug: instead of state being read before an external call completes, state was *not rolled back* when an external call failed. When auditing any deposit/credit flow that triggers a third-party notification or hook, explicitly trace the failure path: if the hook/notification fails, does the code revert the token transfer AND the internal balance/deposit record, or just one of the two? A credited-but-unbacked balance is exploitable the moment it can be withdrawn independently of the asset that was supposed to back it.

**3. Single-key or single-signer mint/release authorization on bridges (Autheo/Hyperlane pattern, standing but freshly illustrated)**

Autheo's Hyperlane Warp Route treated one relayer key (`TrustedRelayerIsm`) as sufficient to authorize minting on the destination chain, with no independent volume cap or secondary signer. For any bridge audit: ask what happens the instant that one key is compromised — is there a per-period mint cap, a multi-sig/multi-relayer quorum requirement, or any check independent of the single signature that would limit blast radius? If the answer is "nothing," that's the finding.

**4. Freshly-deployed consensus/accounting upgrades are a high-risk window (Pirate Chain Ironwood, standing lesson reinforced)**

The Ironwood bug surfaced just 11 days after mainnet activation of the upgrade that introduced it. Any protocol — EVM, UTXO-based, or otherwise — that has shipped a consensus-level or supply-accounting change within the last 2-4 weeks deserves disproportionate scrutiny versus code that's been live for months, simply because it hasn't accumulated the same adversarial attention yet.

**5. Operational/infrastructure compromise, not contract bugs, drives the biggest multi-chain drains (Gamdom, Ledger/CryptoBilis, standing)**

Neither Gamdom's automation-server breach nor the Ledger/CryptoBilis hardware-implant drain involved a smart-contract bug at all — both were key-management/infrastructure compromises. TRM Labs' H1 2026 data (cited in Gamdom coverage) attributes roughly 76% of stolen funds this year to infrastructure/operational compromises rather than contract logic bugs. If you're doing custody, exchange, or wallet-infra security work rather than pure contract audits, treat CI/CD systems, automation/orchestration servers, and hardware supply chains as at least as high-value a target list as the Solidity itself.

**6. Post-mortem latency as its own signal (standing)**

Gamdom, Meter.io, DCENT, Fetch.ai, MultiversX, Blink, MALT, and GoldPesa all currently lack (or substantially lack) a primary technical post-mortem, several well overdue. Reason from on-chain evidence and third-party analysis (Blockaid, SlowMist, Bitquery, PeckShield, Arkham) rather than waiting for an official writeup that may never arrive in useful detail.
