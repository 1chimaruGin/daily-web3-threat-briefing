### Mercado Bitcoin — Brazil's Largest Exchange Hit for ~$46.3M, Root Cause Undisclosed

**Chain:** Multi-chain exchange hot-wallet infrastructure. Secondary reporting (CoinDesk, Altcoin Buzz) describes outflows traced across 10+ blockchains including Ethereum, Solana, Tron, Polygon, Arbitrum, Avalanche, BNB Chain, Stellar and Bitcoin; this series' direct fetch (The Block) cites Arkham data for the dollar figure but does not itself enumerate the chain count, so that detail should be treated as secondary-sourced rather than independently confirmed here.
**Status:** Active investigation. Deposits and withdrawals suspended as a precaution. No root cause, attribution, or compensation plan published as of writing.
**Impact:** Mercado Bitcoin confirmed "approximately $46.3 million in assets" lost, per Arkham data, in a statement reported by The Block (published 3:19pm EST Nov 2). Earlier same-day reporting (CoinDesk) had cited a lower ~$40 million figure before the exchange's own confirmation — treat $46.3M as the tally firming up over the day, not as two separate, additive losses.
**What happened:** Mercado Bitcoin's own statement: "We identified and swiftly contained a security incident in one of our systems early this morning." The exchange said the incident "did not affect customers or result in a data breach." An Arkham analyst quoted by The Block said "the attacker was sweeping funds from older deposit addresses and selling memecoins and other altcoins" — consistent with other reporting's (not independently verified by this series) description of an attacker working through smaller chains before hitting the exchange's main hot wallets.
**Root cause:** Not disclosed. The Block's reporting states only that this was "a security incident in one of our systems," with no technical mechanism, compromised-key claim, or named vulnerability given.
**Remediation:** Deposits and withdrawals suspended while teams "investigate the incident, track the assets, and coordinate with authorities." The exchange published the destination wallet addresses associated with the theft to help security firms track the funds. Tether CEO Paolo Ardoino confirmed Tether froze more than 4.5 million USDT linked to the incident.
**Attribution:** None. Not attributed to any named actor or group, and not established as linked to the Binance hack or any other incident in this series despite the overlapping timeframe — any resemblance is coincidental pending evidence.
**Bug class:** key-management / exchange hot-wallet infrastructure compromise, mechanism undisclosed — could reflect compromised signing keys, compromised internal tooling, or another vector; not yet characterized by the exchange.

### $108.8M (Disputed ~$106M) Multi-Exchange Stablecoin Theft — Kraken, OKX, Crypto.com, Access Vector Unconfirmed

**Chain:** Ethereum (USDC/USDT withdrawals), swapped on-chain via UniswapX into ETH; downstream laundering reportedly aimed at Monero.
**Status:** Active tracing by independent investigators. No exchange-side breach established — this is one customer entity's accounts, compromised across three platforms, not a platform-level hack. Not resolved as of writing.
**Impact:** Two related, non-reconciled figures: ZachXBT's original Nov 1 alert described an unnamed EU-based entity as suspected of losing "more than $106 million"; TokenPost's independent on-chain trace refined this to **$108.8 million** in USDC withdrawn to 15 destination wallets. Treat these as the same underlying event with a tightening estimate, not as additive losses.
**What happened:** $108.8 million in USDC was withdrawn from Ethereum wallets tied to Kraken, OKX and Crypto.com — all apparently controlled by the same EU-based entity — and sent to 15 destination wallets between the first withdrawal (7:25am ET Nov 1, from OKX) and the last arrival (4:09am ET Nov 2). **Kraken accounted for 95% of the total.** Eleven of the 15 destination wallets had received prior payments from the same exchanges, collectively taking 84% of the traced funds; four newly-used wallets received the remaining $17.27 million. Bitquery flagged one wallet receiving 43 separate Kraken withdrawals, each under $50,000, within a 91-minute span — read as the attacker structuring transfers to stay under a per-transaction withdrawal limit. About $62.98 million of the stablecoins were swapped via UniswapX into 27,553 ETH at an average price of ~$2,286/ETH. As of 8:47am ET Nov 2, addresses linked to the suspected attacker still held $78.27 million unmoved, including $3.74 million in USDC.
**Root cause:** Not established. Both TokenPost and Cryptopolitan state explicitly that the trace does not establish how the accounts/wallets were accessed — no confirmed compromised API key, login credential, or private key. The victim entity has not been publicly named.
**Remediation:** None published by Kraken, OKX, or Crypto.com as of writing; no freezes or fund recoveries reported in either source consulted.
**Fund movement:** Per Cryptopolitan's citation of ZachXBT, stolen stablecoins were swapped into ETH almost immediately, then routed through instant-swap services Kyrrex and WellWallet, converted into Monero via a service called Wagyu, and moved further across chains via bridges — a path designed to go dark once funds reach Monero. Investigators have shared eleven addresses linked to the theft.
**Bug class:** key-management / centralized-exchange account-or-credential compromise (access vector unconfirmed) replicated across three independent platforms, compounded by withdrawal-limit-aware structuring to previously-used destination addresses.

### b14g / Core Network — Zero-Value Withdrawal Bug Drains 97M CORE (~$1.47M), 70.2% Clawed Back via Hard Fork

**Chain:** Core blockchain (EVM-compatible L1), affecting b14g, a Bitcoin dual-staking/restaking protocol built on Core and Babylon.
**Status:** Partially remediated via an on-chain hard fork. No user distribution or claims process open yet. Full post-mortem promised, not yet published.
**Impact:** 97,045,563 CORE drained, valued at ~$1.47 million at the time (Bitquery). Of that, **68,146,177.22 CORE (70.2%)** was recovered via a Nov 2 Core network hard fork into a designated recovery address; a separate ~9 million CORE was frozen by exchange partners but has not yet been returned; The Defiant's provisional reconciliation leaves roughly **19.9 million CORE** unaccounted for outside those two categories.
**What happened:** At **09:29 UTC on Oct 31**, an attacker submitted 99 zero-value withdrawal requests against b14g staking positions it had not funded. Core's staking contract (`CoreAgent.sol`) contains a provision that treats a withdrawal request for zero tokens as an instruction to withdraw the delegator's **entire** staked balance — a behavior Bitquery says had been in place for roughly 17 months. b14g's own contract accepted the zero-value requests without verifying the requester actually held a funded position, forwarded them to Core's staking system, and ~97M CORE was released across 91 emptied staking positions in a single transaction. b14g did not pause the affected contracts until **10:35 UTC — about 66 minutes later** — during which the attacker moved 23 million CORE through newly-created intermediary addresses, some touching infrastructure associated with Bybit, Gate.io and MEXC.
**Root cause:** A composability bug at the boundary between Core's staking-contract semantics (zero amount = withdraw-all) and b14g's withdrawal-handling logic, which neither rejected unfunded/unauthorized withdrawal requests nor validated amounts before forwarding them downstream. Bitquery was explicit that it had not reviewed b14g's source code directly and could not identify the precise programming error — this is Bitquery's inference from on-chain behavior and Core's public contract code, not a confirmed root cause from b14g itself. Core DAO stated the underlying blockchain, consensus, and validator-level staking were not compromised; the exploit's scope is limited to CORE deposited through b14g, and BTC staked through the protocol remained time-locked and unaffected.
**Remediation:** Core executed a one-time, consensus-level state adjustment — the "Fermi upgrade," activated **4:00am UTC Nov 2** — that zeroed the balances of three attacker-controlled addresses and credited the combined 68.15M CORE to a recovery address, without reversing transactions, minting new tokens, or changing total supply. b14g paused the affected contracts, is coordinating with Core, exchanges, and law enforcement on the still-frozen ~9M CORE, and said it is "finalizing its distribution plan and timeline" — no claims process is open, and b14g explicitly warned users there is no legitimate recovery service to share private keys or seed phrases with.
**Bug class:** logic-error / unchecked zero-amount withdrawal treated as withdraw-all, compounded by a missing ownership/funding check before forwarding a withdrawal instruction across a cross-contract composability boundary — a textbook unvalidated-sentinel-value bug.

### BTCPay Server — Emergency Shutdown After Hot-Wallet Sweeps on Outdated Instances, Loss Total Unconfirmed

**Chain:** Bitcoin (native on-chain hot wallets managed directly by BTCPay Server's core application — not Lightning/LND).
**Status:** Active investigation. Operators told to shut down affected instances. No patch, confirmed loss total, or affected-server count published as of writing.
**Impact:** Unconfirmed and unquantified. BTCPay Server has not disclosed a loss total, a count of affected installations, or a count of user-reported losses — this is explicitly an open incident without a confirmed dollar figure, not a quiet one.
**What happened:** On **Nov 1**, BTCPay Server posted publicly: "We are receiving reports of hot wallets on BTCPay Servers being swept by unauthorized attackers," instructing operators to run `./btcpay-down.sh` and remain offline pending investigation. A **Nov 2** follow-up from founder Nicolas Dorier narrowed the active-exploitation window to instances running **version 2.4.1 or earlier** (tagged July 23, 2026), and said attackers had logged in **as administrators** on affected servers — but investigators had not established how that administrator access was obtained; stolen credentials, a software flaw, or another route all remain open possibilities. BTCPay's own public-facing servers, running the current 2.4.7, were not attacked, though the team cautioned this doesn't prove every updated installation is safe, and the shutdown instruction was not limited to any specific version.
**Root cause:** Not established. Administrator-level access preceded the sweeps; the entry vector is unconfirmed. ## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Zero/sentinel-value semantics at cross-contract composability boundaries (b14g/Core, new this window)**

b14g's attacker didn't find a reentrancy bug or a signature flaw — they found that Core's staking layer treats a withdrawal request for *zero* tokens as "withdraw everything," and that b14g's own contract forwarded attacker-supplied withdrawal requests without checking the requester actually held a funded position. This bug existed in Core's public contract code for roughly 17 months before anyone (publicly) exploited it. For audit/bounty work: explicitly test boundary and sentinel values (0, max-uint, negative-where-unsigned-wraps, empty arrays) against every contract you call into, not just your own — and treat "does the caller verify funded ownership before forwarding a request downstream" as a required check at every composability boundary, especially where a dependency's documented behavior diverges from the intuitive reading of a parameter name.

**2. Reused withdrawal addresses and sub-limit structuring across multiple exchange accounts controlled by one entity (Kraken/OKX/Crypto.com theft, new this window)**

Whoever compromised this EU-based entity's accounts moved $108.8M by withdrawing to 15 destinations, 11 of which had legitimate withdrawal history with the same exchanges — and by splitting one transfer into 43 sub-$50,000 withdrawals over 91 minutes to apparently dodge a per-transaction limit. If you're testing exchange-side withdrawal infrastructure under a bounty scope, this is a concrete pattern to probe: does anomaly detection catch a sudden burst of maximum-frequency, just-under-limit withdrawals to addresses that have *previously* received legitimate payments, especially when the same pattern repeats across more than one platform for the same underlying customer?

**3. Self-hosted hot-wallet software left unpatched is a live, exploited attack surface (BTCPay Server, new this window)**

The fix (2.4.2+) existed before the thefts; the exposure was operators who deployed once and never upgraded, then had their admin access compromised through a still-unconfirmed route. For any audit or bounty scope touching self-hosted or on-prem wallet software, explicitly model "what does an attacker get from an outdated, never-updated deployment with admin-level access" as part of the threat model — not just the latest release's code path — since that is evidently where real-world attackers are currently looking.

**4. Undisclosed root cause on a hot-wallet sweep doesn't mean "no bug" — it means the bug hasn't been named yet (Mercado Bitcoin, new this window)**

Mercado Bitcoin's ~$46.3M loss, like BTCPay Server's, is still an open incident with no named technical mechanism. Both joined Binance this same week in putting exchange-side signing infrastructure, internal tooling, and key-management processes around hot wallets back in the spotlight as the most consequential and often least-audited part of a centralized platform's stack. Worth treating "how are hot-wallet signing keys provisioned, rotated, and access-controlled internally" as in-scope wherever a program's bounty terms allow infrastructure-adjacent findings, not just on-chain contract code.
