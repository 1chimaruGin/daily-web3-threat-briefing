# Web3 Threat Briefing — 2026-09-20

*Reconstructed on 2026-09-21 from archived sources; written after the fact, not same-day.*

> **Scope:** Multi-chain (EVM/Solidity · Solana/Rust · Cosmos/Move · cross-chain bridges). Priority: DeFi, wallets, key infrastructure.
> **Window:** Last ~24–48h (September 19–20 UTC). **Headline: Three incidents in 48 hours, all landing on Sept 19, and none of them a Solidity bug. ## 2. 🟠 Ongoing — carried forward

### Fetch.ai (FET) + NuNet (NTX) — Stolen Bridge Signing Key, ~$2.01M (Day 2)

**Chain:** Ethereum
**Status:** CONFIRMED. The drain landed on **Sept 19** — Blockaid and PeckShield both date the attack to the 19th, and the earliest write-up carrying that attribution published at 03:12 UTC on the 20th.
**Sept 20 developments (why it is still here):** Fetch.ai published its **preliminary on-chain analysis**, naming a compromised SingularityNET bridge backend signing key as the entry point; jointly with SingularityNET it **deactivated the affected wallets and contracts**; SlowMist published the `conversionIn`/`conversionOut` modifier asymmetry; and NTX printed its all-time low. No compensation or remediation framework published at time of reporting.
**Impact:** ~$2.01M combined — **8,721,530 FET (~$1.55–1.56M)** drained from Fetch.ai's converter, plus **408.5M NTX (~$452K, roughly 42% of total NTX supply)** minted on the NuNet side.

**Recap:** A single wallet cluster (reported as `0x1572…c362`) hit both protocols within a very short window — the KuCoin/PANews summaries of Fetch.ai's own analysis describe both legs landing **within one minute**. The FET leg drained the remaining inventory of the `TokenConversionManagerV3` contract via its `conversionIn` function in a single transaction. The attacker did not break cryptography or find a classic contract bug: they held a **valid conversion-authorizer signature**, traced by Fetch.ai to a compromised **backend signing key for the SingularityNET cross-chain bridge**. The NuNet leg used a stolen **minting key** on the NuNet deployer account.

Proceeds were converted to ETH — reported as 546.36 ETH (~$1.44M) at the time of the Cryptonomist write-up, and 547.89 ETH plus 230M NTX still held in the attacker wallet as of Fetch.ai's report.

**Market impact:** NTX fell 70%+ to an all-time low of $0.000328; FET fell roughly 5%. (Some outlets reported NTX down as much as 95%; the exact drawdown figure is inconsistent across reporting and should be treated as unconfirmed.)

**Root cause:** Off-chain key compromise, amplified by a weak on-chain authorization design. Per SlowMist's analysis, `TokenConversionManagerV3.conversionIn()` left a **single-EOA ECDSA signature as its sole authorization check** and, critically, **omitted the `checkLimits(amount)` modifier that the corresponding `conversionOut()` function does apply**. With no rate limit, no per-transaction cap and no second on-chain validation layer, one leaked key converted directly into a full-inventory drain in one transaction instead of a series of capped withdrawals someone might have caught.

**Bug class:** key-management (compromised bridge signing key), access-control (asymmetric authorization — limits enforced on one direction of the bridge but not the other)

### MultiversX — VM-Level Atomicity Flaw, Mainnet Halted (Day 2)

**Chain:** MultiversX (EGLD)
**Status:** NETWORK HALTED — block production still paused. The attack and the first "anomalies" notice are **Sept 19** events (TechFlow timestamps the initial emergency-investigation post at 10:16 on Sept 19 UTC+8, and SlowMist dates the incident 9/19). Loss amount **not disclosed**.
**Sept 20 developments (why it is still here):** the team published its **confirmation** that an attacker "attempted to exploit virtual machine-level atomicity issues, causing invalid state changes"; a patch is now being **validated on a shadow fork**; a **targeted recovery** preserving finalized history was floated; and the status page showed public API, xPortal, Explorer, Wallet, Bridge, xExchange and xLaunchpad degraded.

**Recap:** an actor exploited an **atomicity issue at the mainnet virtual-machine level** on Sept 19, causing invalid state changes to be written on-chain. The team paused the network to contain further impact rather than let the corrupted state propagate, and confirmed the cause publicly on Sept 20.

Engineers prepared a patch and are validating it in a shadow-fork environment before mainnet deployment. The team is evaluating a **targeted recovery** that would preserve finalized transaction history and legitimate user state while reversing only the incident-related invalid changes — i.e. a surgical state repair rather than a full rollback. Users were told not to submit or rebroadcast transactions and not to deposit or withdraw EGLD or ESDT via exchanges or bridges. A technical report is promised once the incident closes.

**Root cause:** Not yet publicly detailed beyond "VM-level atomicity." The class is a **client/VM implementation bug**: an execution path where a multi-step operation was not applied all-or-nothing, so a partially-applied state transition was committed as valid. Do not assume a specific mechanism until MultiversX publishes the technical report.

**Bug class:** logic-error / client-implementation (non-atomic state transition at the VM layer)

### Blink Wallet — Custodial Account Breach, Amount Undisclosed (Day 2)

**Chain:** Bitcoin / Lightning Network
**Status:** Services were fully suspended on Sept 19; reporting indicates services were subsequently restored after a patch was deployed and verified, while **impacted users remained locked out**. **Total amount withdrawn has not been disclosed and no technical post-mortem has been published.**

**What happened:** Blink, a Bitcoin Lightning payments app, shut down all services on Sept 19 after discovering that an attacker had accessed a subset of its **custodial** accounts and withdrawn funds. Reporting describes "a few dozen" custodial accounts affected. **Non-custodial wallets — where users hold their own keys — were not affected**, and Blink stated the Spark protocol was unaffected.

Blink said the majority of funds remain secure, pointing to a multisig cold-storage design paired with hot-wallet components that was intended to cap exposure in exactly this scenario. The company said it had identified all affected accounts and that every affected user would be reimbursed.

**Root cause:** Under investigation. Blink deployed a patch, which suggests the team identified at least the surface-level exploit path, but **the precise breach vector has not been published**. Nothing in the public record yet establishes whether this was credential compromise, an authorization flaw in the withdrawal path, or infrastructure access — treat any specific claim as unconfirmed.

**Bug class:** access-control / key-management (custodial infrastructure — exact vector unconfirmed)

**Sources:**

### Nostra Finance — NSTR Oracle Manipulation, $3.5M (Day 4)

**Chain:** Starknet
**Status:** MONEY MARKET FULLY PAUSED (Day 4) — supply, borrow, withdrawal and liquidation all disabled. Nostra stated: *"We are reconciling the impact on each asset and tracing the funds. The final loss and potential recoveries are not yet known."* Recovery prospects unclear.
**Impact:** ~$3.5M borrowed against worthless collateral.

**What happened (Sept 17):** The attacker created a **fake NSTR/SolvBTC liquidity pool** seeded with minimal one-sided liquidity (reported as 1.5 SolvBTC), then wash-traded it so that **GeckoTerminal's pool-selection logic** picked the rigged pool as the reference source for NSTR's price. NSTR's reported price went from ~$0.006 to ~$49.50 — roughly an **8,000×** move. The attacker then supplied inflated NSTR as collateral and borrowed ETH, STRK, USDC, USDT, WBTC and DAI out of the money market.

The killer detail: **NSTR's entire circulating market cap was roughly $550K–$590K**, yet the borrow exceeded that by more than five times. Any sanity check tying a listed collateral's borrow cap to its total market cap would have stopped this.

**Fund movement:** Per PeckShield and CertiK, ~$1.92M moved to Ethereum mainnet (234.57 ETH and 1.3M DAI), with ~$1.55M still traceable on Starknet. Funds were routed through AVNU, Ekubo and JediSwap, and ~2.2M STRK left Starknet entirely via the NEAR Intents bridge.

**Unconfirmed:** the exact oracle provider feeding NSTR at the time of the attack. Nostra integrated Chainlink Price Feeds in May 2026 but NSTR was not named in that announcement, and whether the NSTR feed was Chainlink, Pragma or another source has not been clarified publicly.

**Bug class:** price-manipulation (DEX-pool-derived oracle with attacker-creatable reference pool; no market-cap-relative borrow cap)

### DCENT App Wallet — Signing Vulnerability (Day 6)

**Chain:** Multi-chain — Bitcoin, Ethereum and EVM chains, XRPL, TRON and others
**Status:** ONGOING (Day 6, counted from the **Sept 15** drain; the vendor's public alert followed on Sept 16). Root cause **deliberately withheld**.
**Impact:** Between **16:29 and 18:34 UTC on Sept 15**, attackers reportedly drained 1,552 wallets of **2,009,321 XRP** (worth over $2.8M at the time). Losses on other chains have not been quantified publicly.
**Sept 20 revision (why it is still here):** the XRP figure was **revised sharply upward** — reporting now puts the theft at roughly **$9.3M across 6,160 addresses**, against the ~$2.8M / 1,552-wallet figure that circulated from the 17th through the 19th. DCENT also moved its update instruction to **v10.0.0 or later**. Both numbers are third-party; DCENT has still published no loss figure of its own.

**Recap:** DCENT issued an urgent alert on Sept 16 after detecting abnormal asset transfers from its **App Wallet** (software wallet) mode. The company's preliminary incident report titles the issue an **"App Wallet Signing Vulnerability"** but explicitly declines to publish the mechanism, stating that *"if specific technical information were made public, it could be used in identical or similar attacks or lead to further harm. To prevent that, we are not disclosing at this time technical details that could affect the investigation or the security response."*

**Scope, per DCENT's own report:**

| Condition | Affected? |
|---|---|
| App versions **earlier than 8.1.0** (released Nov 5, 2025) | **Yes** |
| Recovery phrase ever entered into the App Wallet, with signing history | **Yes — move funds now** |
| Recovery phrase reused/imported into a DCENT hardware wallet | **Yes** |
| Hardware device connected only, phrase never entered in the app | No action required |

**Remediation per vendor:** update to the latest app version, generate a **new wallet with a fresh recovery phrase**, move assets to the new address, and do not reuse previous addresses. DCENT says it is working with law enforcement, security specialists and exchanges to trace and freeze funds.

**Note on inference:** "signing vulnerability" + "history of signing transactions" + multi-chain scope is consistent with a nonce/entropy weakness in the app's signing path that leaks key material across signatures — but DCENT has **not** confirmed this, and the specific mechanism is unconfirmed.

### Liquid Network / Blockstream — Elements Range-Proof Cache Bug (Day 14)

**Chain:** Liquid (Bitcoin sidechain)
**Status:** PARTIAL RECOVERY (Day 14, counted from the Sept 7 public disclosure). Block production resumed **Sept 10**, initially without transactions as a precaution. **Peg operations — including peg-ins and PAK-authorized peg-outs — remained suspended** while the BTC/L-BTC reserve is restored.
**Impact:** ~4,000 BTC (~$320M at the time) drained from the federation wallet on **Sept 6 at 15:53:10 UTC (Liquid block 4,050,336)**. **3,400 BTC returned**; approximately **598.5 BTC (~$47M) remains outstanding** with the actors.

**Root cause (now public and patchable):** a vulnerability in the open-source **Elements** software in how Liquid nodes **cache range-proof verifications**. The cache keys were insufficiently specific, allowing a proof result to be reused where it should not have been — which let the attacker mint **unbacked L-BTC** that the sidechain accepted as valid, then exchange it for real BTC.

**The fix is a verifiable primary source:** Elements **v23.3.4**, released **Sept 9, 2026**, whose release notes include *"sigcache: harden range proof cache keys and add `-norangeproofcache` option"* (PR #1600). Functionary nodes were updated immediately.

**Attribution note:** the actors self-identified as white hats and returned most of the funds after Blockstream signed an on-chain note confirming bridge nodes were patched. Blockstream has described them as *"purported"* white-hats. Retaining ~598 BTC is not consistent with a white-hat return, and several commentators characterised it as closer to extortion. **Treat the white-hat framing as the actors' claim, not a confirmed fact.**

**Bug class:** logic-error / client-implementation (verification-cache key collision permitting unbacked issuance)

## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

**No new web3-relevant CVEs were identified in the Sept 19–20 window.** The actionable advisory-grade items below are technique write-ups, vendor releases and vendor reports rather than CVE-numbered disclosures.

### Asymmetric Authorization Across Bridge Directions — the Fetch.ai Pattern

**Pattern:** The single most reusable finding from the Fetch.ai incident is not "a key leaked." Keys leak constantly. It is that **`conversionIn()` and `conversionOut()` on the same contract did not enforce the same guards.** `conversionOut()` applied a `checkLimits(amount)` modifier. `conversionIn()` did not. So the inbound path — the one that releases inventory — was the *less* protected of the two, and a single leaked EOA key converted straight into a full-reserve drain in one transaction.

**Why this matters for hunters:** bridge and converter contracts almost always have paired functions. Reviewers read them as a pair conceptually but audit them one at a time, and modifier drift between the two is easy to miss in a diff-less read. The severity multiplier is enormous: with a per-transaction cap, a leaked key yields a slow, noisy, monitorable bleed that operators can catch and pause. Without one, it yields a single atomic drain.

**Hunting checklist:**
- For every bridge/converter/minter contract, build a literal table: function name → every modifier applied → every `require` on amount → every signer check. Any row that is a strict subset of its counterpart's row is a finding.
- Does the signature check verify against a **single EOA**, or a multisig / threshold scheme? A single-EOA authorizer on a path that releases the full reserve is a critical-severity design finding on its own, independent of whether the key has leaked.
- Is there a **rate limit or per-tx cap on the release path**, not just the deposit path? Rate limits are the control that converts a total loss into a partial loss when — not if — a key leaks.
- Is the signature **replay-bound** (nonce, deadline, chainId, contract address in the digest)? A valid-but-old authorizer signature is as good as a stolen key.

**Bug class:** access-control, key-management

**Sources:** [CoinOtag technical breakdown](https://en.coinotag.com/fetch-ai-fet-exploit-1-56m-token-converter-drain); [Fetch.ai preliminary on-chain analysis via PANews](https://panews.io/articles/01a0be17-d634-74c1-9844-959b074bea73)

### SlowMist: Full-Chain iOS Exploit Draining Wallet Keys (disclosed Sept 19)

**What was disclosed:** On **Sept 19**, SlowMist CISO 23pds warned that attackers have operationalised a **full-chain iOS exploit** capable of silently extracting private keys and recovery phrases from crypto wallets on affected devices.

**Reported attack chain:** target visits a malicious webpage in Safari (typically via social engineering) → memory-corruption bug in **WebKit / JavaScriptCore** yields arbitrary read/write → **Pointer Authentication Code (PAC) bypass** → escape from the WebContent sandbox → kernel-level privilege escalation → access to device **Keychain** data. Keys and seed phrases are lifted directly off the device, so there is no password-reset or account-recovery step for the attacker to trip over and no server-side signal for the wallet vendor to alarm on.

**Reported affected range:** iOS 13 through iOS 26.5, with the upper bound described as **pending final confirmation**.

**Confirmed vs unconfirmed:** the disclosure was a researcher post on X, not a formal advisory. **No CVE has been cited and SlowMist had not published a longer formal advisory at time of reporting.** The specific affected-version ceiling in particular should be treated as unconfirmed.

**Mitigation:** update iOS immediately; avoid unsolicited links in Safari; if a hot wallet may have been on an exposed device, **generate new keys on a clean, updated device and migrate funds** — rotating the app is not sufficient, the seed is the compromised artifact.

**Bug class:** key-management (client platform compromise)

**Source:** [BlockchainReporter — SlowMist Warns Of iOS Exploit Stealing Wallet Keys](https://blockchainreporter.net/slowmist-warns-of-ios-exploit-stealing-wallet-keys)

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Modifier-parity sweep on every paired bridge function — do this first, it's cheap**

The Fetch.ai loss came down to one missing modifier on one of two mirrored functions. This is mechanically greppable and almost nobody does it systematically. For each target bridge/converter/vault:

- Enumerate the mirrored pairs: `conversionIn`/`conversionOut`, `deposit`/`withdraw`, `lock`/`unlock`, `mint`/`burn`, `bridgeIn`/`bridgeOut`.
- Diff their modifier lists, amount `require`s, pause checks and signer checks side by side.
- Any asymmetry is a finding. Weight it by which direction moves value *out* of the protocol — that's the one that pays.
- Then ask the severity-amplifier question separately: **if the authorizer key leaked tomorrow, what is the maximum single-transaction loss?** If the answer is "the entire reserve," the missing rate limit is itself a submittable design finding even with no key compromise, because it converts a foreseeable operational failure into a total one.

**2. Collateral listings where borrow capacity exceeds token market cap**

Nostra's attacker borrowed ~$3.5M against a token whose entire circulating market cap was under $600K. That ratio is the finding, and it is checkable from public data without reading a line of code:

- For each listed collateral on a lending market, compute `max_borrowable_against_asset` vs `asset_circulating_market_cap`. Any asset where the protocol will lend out more than the token is collectively worth is pre-broken.
- Then check the oracle: is the price derived from **DEX pools an attacker can create**? Aggregators that auto-select the "best" pool (GeckoTerminal-style pool-selection logic was the specific vector here) are exploitable by spinning up a fake pair with thin one-sided liquidity and wash-trading it into the reference slot.
- Look for the gap between a protocol's *announced* oracle integration and its *actual* per-asset feeds. Nostra announced Chainlink Price Feeds in May 2026; NSTR was not in that announcement. **Long-tail governance tokens are routinely the assets left on the old, weaker feed after a headline oracle upgrade** — read the integration announcement, then enumerate which listed assets it did *not* cover.

**3. VM / client-layer atomicity, not just contract logic**

Two of this window's incidents (MultiversX, Liquid) were bugs in node software, not in Solidity. Both halted or degraded an entire chain. If your target scope includes node or VM code:

- Hunt multi-step state transitions that are not applied all-or-nothing: partial writes on a revert path, state committed before a later validation fails, cross-shard or cross-contract calls whose failure does not roll back the caller's effects.
- Hunt **verification caches** specifically. The Liquid bug was a cache key that was not specific enough, so a proof verification result was reused in a context where it did not hold. Any `sigcache`, proof cache or memoised validator is a candidate: enumerate every input the verification actually depends on, then check that **every one of them is in the cache key**. A missing field in a cache key is a silent verification bypass.
- These bugs are underhunted relative to their impact because they require reading Go/C++/Rust node code rather than Solidity, and they pay out at chain-halt severity.

**4. Custodial-side surfaces on non-custodial-branded products**

Blink's non-custodial wallets were fine; its custodial accounts were drained. Many wallet products carry both modes under one brand, and the custodial half is ordinary web infrastructure with crypto-sized payouts:

- In any bounty scope that includes a wallet app, check whether custodial balances, exchange-mode accounts or hosted-Lightning accounts are in scope — they often are, and they attract far less hunter attention than the contracts.
- Target the withdrawal authorization path: can a session/API token be issued or reused without re-authentication? Are withdrawal destinations bound to the authenticated session? Are hot-wallet withdrawal limits enforced server-side per-account *and* globally?
- The same logic applies to DCENT: the compromised surface was the **software** wallet mode inside a hardware-wallet company's app. The brand's security reputation rests on the hardware; the losses came from the app.
