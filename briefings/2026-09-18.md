## 1. 🔴 Active exploits (last 24h)

### Nostra Finance — NSTR Oracle Price Manipulation (Starknet, Sept 17 — Day 2)

**Chain:** Starknet
**Status:** MONEY MARKET FULLY PAUSED — supply, borrow, withdrawal and liquidation all disabled. Pool-by-pool reconciliation underway. Nostra has stated that the final loss figure and any recoveries are **not yet known**.
**Impact:** ~$3.5M borrowed out in ETH, STRK, USDC, USDT, WBTC and DAI. DefiLlama showed Nostra TVL falling from roughly $4M on Sept 16 to roughly $710K.

**What happened (timeline, all UTC, Sept 17):**

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| 05:23 | Attacker creates a fake NSTR/SolvBTC pool seeded with ~1.5 SolvBTC of one-sided liquidity |
| 05:27–05:47 | Wash trades routed through the thin pool; liquidity pulled out of the market-making range |
| 05:47–05:48 | Repeated swaps print NSTR at ~$49.50, up from ~$0.006 — roughly 8,000x |
| 05:48–05:50 | Inflated NSTR posted as collateral; ETH, STRK, USDC, USDT, WBTC, DAI borrowed (~$3.5M) |
| 05:51–07:08 | Proceeds liquidated across Starknet DEXs (AVNU, Ekubo, JediSwap); ~2.2M STRK bridged via NEAR Intents |
| 13:28 | Nostra confirms the incident publicly and pauses the money market |

**Sept 18 developments:**
- **00:41 UTC** — PeckShield reported ~$1.92M moved to Ethereum mainnet: 234.57 ETH and 1.3M DAI. CertiK separately tracked the same consolidation; roughly $1.55M was reported still sitting on Starknet at that point.
- SlowMist classified the event as oracle manipulation and stated final losses and recoveries remained unconfirmed.
- Nostra warned users about phishing follow-on, saying it would never DM users or ask them to connect wallets.

**Root cause:** A *pricing-authority* failure, not a contract bug. The lending market accepted an aggregator-selected spot price for NSTR. Community and vendor reconstructions attribute the pool-selection step to GeckoTerminal's ranking logic, which favours surface signals (recent volume, displayed liquidity) over pool depth — so a freshly created, near-empty NSTR/SolvBTC pair became the reference source. Once it was the reference, a few thousand dollars of wash trading was enough to move the mark. The underlying economics: **NSTR's entire circulating market cap was roughly $546K–$590K**, and the protocol let it back a borrow more than 5x that size. There was no TWAP, no multi-source deviation circuit breaker, no borrow cap indexed to float, and no collateral isolation for the governance token.

> **Unconfirmed:** the exact oracle provider Nostra used for NSTR has not been confirmed. Nostra integrated Chainlink Price Feeds in May 2026 for several assets, but NSTR was not on that initial list. The minute-level timeline above comes from third-party reconstructions, not from an official Nostra post-mortem — no post-mortem had been published as of Sept 18.

**Bug class:** oracle-manipulation / price-manipulation (aggregator-sourced spot price + low-float collateral)

## 2. 🟠 Ongoing — carried forward

### DCENT App Wallet — Signing Vulnerability / Mass Key Compromise (Day 4)

**Chains:** XRP Ledger (confirmed drains), plus Bitcoin, Ethereum, TRON and EVM chains flagged as in-scope by the vendor
**Status:** ACTIVE / UNRESOLVED. DCENT published a *Preliminary Incident Report* dated **September 17 (KST)** and is explicitly **withholding the technical root cause**, stating that disclosure "could be used in identical or similar attacks or lead to further harm."
**Impact as reported on Sept 18:** ~2,009,321 XRP (~$2.8M at the time) drained from 1,552 wallets in a 2h05m window, 16:29–18:34 UTC. SlowMist's archive lists the DCENT entry at 9/15 and $6.57M multi-chain.

> **Date — settled.** The drain ran on **Sept 15 UTC**, 16:29–18:34, and the Day count here runs from that date. On-chain reconstruction of the XRP sweep, SlowMist's archive entry and DCENT's own account all agree: the vendor's first unauthorized-transfer report arrived "early morning Sept 16 KST," which is 01:29–03:34 KST on the 16th — i.e. immediately after a drain that ended 18:34 UTC on the 15th. Public disclosure followed on **Sept 16**, the preliminary incident report on **Sept 17 KST**. The loss figure is also unsettled: ~$2.8M was the figure circulating on Sept 18; by Sept 20 it had been revised to ~$9.3M across 6,160 addresses. Do not treat the higher number as known on the 18th.

**Affected scope (per vendor):** DCENT App Wallet (the software wallet) **versions earlier than 8.1.0** (released 2025-11-05), on Android and iOS. Wallets are in scope if a recovery phrase was entered into the App Wallet **and** the address has a history of *signing* — transfers, approvals, NFT actions, dApp interactions. Receive-only addresses are lower risk but not excluded. Hardware-wallet users who only connected the device and never typed the seed into the app are unaffected.

**Why "signing" matters:** the vendor titled its own report *App Wallet Signing Vulnerability* and scoped exposure to addresses with signing history rather than to all addresses holding a seed. That pattern is what you would expect from a flaw in signature nonce generation or key derivation on the signing path — i.e. keys recoverable from published signatures — rather than from server-side seed exfiltration. **This is inference, not confirmation.** DCENT has not said which it is. Corroborating evidence for pre-compromised keys: analysis of the XRP drain found the attackers worked from a pre-compiled victim list ordered by account creation date, not balance, meaning they were not scanning the ledger live.

**Remediation:** update to the latest app version from official stores only, generate a **new** recovery phrase, move all coins, tokens, NFTs and approvals to the new address, never reuse the old one.

**Bug class:** key-management (signing-path flaw, exact mechanism undisclosed)

### Flamincome / FlamingoFinance — Vault Share-Price Inflation (Day 3)

**Chain:** listed as multi-chain in SlowMist's archive; the on-chain trail cited in reporting is Ethereum
**Status:** No official protocol statement. The affected contracts are described as **older Flamincome deployments**, apparently deprecated but still live.
**Impact:** ~$345,900 attacker profit in USDT.

**Mechanism:** the attacker flash-loaned ~$18M USDT, staked USDP LP tokens into a strategy contract to inflate the reported share price of `VaultYUSDT`, then redeemed liquid aUSDT at the inflated rate. Classic share-price / exchange-rate inflation against a vault whose price-per-share is derived from a balance an attacker can temporarily donate into. Blockaid's exploit-detection system flagged it and published wallet attribution: one attacker address received 0.1 ETH from Tornado Cash ahead of the exploit, then later moved 144.15 ETH and interacted with LI.FI.

**Bug class:** share-price-inflation / unsafe asset accounting, amplified by flash-loan

### Liquid Network (Blockstream) — $320M Range-Proof Cache Collision (Day 12)

**Chain:** Liquid (Bitcoin sidechain)
**Status:** PARTIAL RECOVERY. Block production and ordinary transactions resumed Sept 10. **Peg-ins and peg-outs remain suspended** as of Sept 18, with no published restart date. No new Liquid development was reported in the Sept 17–18 window — this entry is state-of-play, not news.
**Outstanding:** 598.5 BTC (~$47M) never returned.

**Recap:** On Sept 6, 2026 an attacker exploited a range-proof verification **cache-key collision** in Elements, the software powering Liquid. The cache key omitted asset and script context, so a proof already verified in one context validated a fresh issuance — minting ~4,000 unbacked L-BTC, which were redeemed for real BTC. Federation reserves fell from ~4,205 BTC to 197 BTC. **No federation member private keys were compromised.** The attacker returned ~3,400 BTC on Sept 7 after claiming white-hat status via an on-chain OP_RETURN, then demanded 10% as a "bug bounty"; Blockstream publicly refused on Sept 11 ("We will not pay for the return of stolen property") and referred the matter to law enforcement.

**Reserve position (last published figure, Sept 10 22:55 UTC):** 4,229.33 L-BTC outstanding against 3,601.47 BTC in the federation reserve — **85.15% coverage**, a gap of ~627.85 BTC. Adam Back has said the 1:1 peg will be covered. With peg-outs disabled, secondary-market L-BTC price does not guarantee redemption at par.

**Patch:** Elements v23.3.4, deployed Sept 9.

**Bug class:** logic-error (verification-cache key omitting security-relevant context — a validation-cache poisoning pattern)

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Low-float governance tokens used as collateral — check borrow cap against circulating market cap**

Nostra is the cleanest possible instance: a token with a ~$550K free float backed a $3.5M borrow. The attack required no contract bug at all. For any lending market in scope, build the table yourself:

- For every listed collateral asset, compute `max_borrowable_against_asset` vs the asset's **free-float** market cap (not FDV).
- Any asset where borrowable exceeds float is a standing invitation. Anything within ~2x of float is in range for a funded attacker.
- Check whether the protocol's own token is listed as collateral. It usually is, it usually has the thinnest real liquidity, and it is usually the one nobody re-reviewed after launch.
- Check for collateral isolation: can the manipulated asset back borrows of *unrelated* blue-chip assets, or is it siloed?

**2. Aggregator-sourced price feeds — attack the pool-selection step, not the price**

The interesting move at Nostra was not the wash trade, it was **becoming the reference pool**. If a target reads prices from a data aggregator (GeckoTerminal, DEX Screener, CoinGecko on-chain endpoints) rather than from a hardened oracle, the selection heuristic is the attack surface:

- What ranks a pool as canonical — recent volume? displayed liquidity? pair creation order? Each of those is cheap to fake.
- Can an attacker *create* a new pool for the asset, against any quote token, and have it picked up? Is there a minimum-depth floor, a minimum-age requirement, or an allowlist of pool addresses?
- Is there any deviation circuit breaker — a hard reject on an N% move between updates — or does the protocol consume whatever it is handed?
- Is there a TWAP, or is it a spot read? A single-block spot read from an attacker-creatable pool is a total compromise of the lending market.

Note which assets on a target use the aggregator path versus a Chainlink/Pyth path. Mixed configurations are common and the aggregator-path assets are where to spend your time.

**3. Deprecated-but-live vault deployments (the Flamincome pattern)**

The Flamincome loss came from *older* contracts still holding funds and still accepting calls. Two separate hunts:

- **Find the orphans.** Check the target's full deployment history — old factories, v1 vaults, migration leftovers. Contracts that no longer appear in the frontend are still callable and are almost never re-audited, yet they often still hold real balances.
- **Attack price-per-share.** For any ERC-4626-style or vault-share accounting contract, the question is whether `pricePerShare` can be moved within a transaction by an action an attacker controls — a direct token donation, an LP stake into a strategy contract, a rewards claim. Then: does redemption in the same transaction settle at the inflated rate? Flash loans make the capital requirement irrelevant, so the only real defence is either virtual shares/offset accounting or a share price that cannot be moved by an unsolicited balance change.

**4. Audit the signing path in mobile wallets, not just the storage path**

DCENT scoped its exposure to addresses with *signing history*, and the attackers worked from a pre-built victim list rather than scanning the ledger. That combination points at keys being derivable from published artifacts. If you review wallet code:

- Is ECDSA nonce generation deterministic per RFC 6979, or does it draw from a platform RNG? If the latter, what happens on a cold start, in a restored-from-backup state, or on a device where the RNG is not yet seeded?
- Is the same nonce reachable for two different messages across chains that share a key (e.g. secp256k1 reused across BTC/ETH/TRON)? Cross-chain key reuse turns a single nonce collision into a full compromise of every chain.
- Is key derivation deterministic in a way an attacker can precompute given only a public identifier — installation ID, timestamp, account creation ordinal? The victim list being ordered by *account creation date* is the tell worth chasing.
- Version boundaries matter: DCENT's cutoff is app < 8.1.0. When a vendor names a version boundary, diff the release.
