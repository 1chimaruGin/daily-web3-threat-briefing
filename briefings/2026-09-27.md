# Web3 Threat Briefing — 2026-09-27

> **Scope:** Multi-chain (EVM/Solidity · Solana/Rust · Cosmos/Move · cross-chain bridges). Priority: DeFi, wallets, key infrastructure.
> **Window:** Last ~24–48h (September 25–27 UTC). **Headline: a fresh bug class for this series — a meta-transaction "trusted forwarder" flaw in Limit Break's Payment Processor V2/V3 let an attacker impersonate any wallet holding a live NFT/token operator approval as a trade counterparty, without that wallet signing anything. A white-hat rescue moved 23,155 NFTs to safety, but the real theft figure is genuinely disputed between sources, and the underlying contract (V2) has no pause mechanism — it stays exploitable forever on every chain it's deployed on. Meanwhile Bitget locked in a withdrawal-resumption schedule without ever publishing a full root-cause report, and the XRPL's post-incident signature fix slipped its activation date.**

## 1. 🔴 Active exploits (last 24h)

### Magic Eden / Limit Break — Payment Processor V2 Meta-Transaction Spoofing, disputed ~$1.4-2.8M stolen (+ ~$5.7M rescued)

**Chain:** Ethereum (primary), also Polygon, Base, Arbitrum and ApeChain — anywhere Limit Break's Payment Processor V2/V3 is deployed. The exposure traces back to Magic Eden's EVM NFT marketplace, which used Payment Processor V2 to settle trades until it switched away in October 2024 and shut the EVM marketplace down entirely in Q1 2026 — meaning the vulnerable approvals are years-old and mostly forgotten by their owners.
**Status:** PARTIALLY CONTAINED. First malicious transfers detected around **Sept 24, 13:08 UTC** (an initial small drain of ~305 NFTs); the broader exploit was flagged by researcher "Cirrus" at **06:31:42 UTC on Sept 25** and confirmed active through at least **Sept 26**. Limit Break paused **Payment Processor V3** (which shares the same bug) on every chain it could — reportedly excluding ApeChain, where a differing fee configuration is said to keep it usable until end-November. **Payment Processor V2 has no pause or upgrade mechanism at all**, so it remains permanently exploitable regardless of any fix Limit Break makes elsewhere. Magic Eden says "no live Magic Eden listings were impacted" since it stopped using V2 in 2024, but any wallet that once granted it an operator approval and never revoked it is still exposed today.
**Impact:** Genuinely disputed across sources — treat all figures as ranges, not confirmed totals. Yuga Labs VP **0xQuit** ran a white-hat rescue operation overnight Sept 24–25 and moved **23,155 NFTs** (his own valuation: "worth more than $5.7M") into a custody wallet so their owners can reclaim them after revoking the old approval — this is defensive, not stolen, value. Separately, real attacker theft is reported inconsistently: 0xQuit says **660 WETH (~$1.7M)** "I wasn't fast enough for"; on-chain tracker CryptoTicker counted **~530.7–534.7 WETH from 911–936 wallets (~$1.43M)** plus **8,380 USDC from 62 wallets** and **~549,000 WILD** tokens, and separately flagged **7,870 transfers from 1,786 wallets** to addresses not yet attributed; one outlet's rollup put total cross-chain damage at "at least $2.8 million." Revoke.cash's own incident-tracking page reportedly still listed **$0 confirmed stolen** as of the time of writing — likely a methodology/indexing lag rather than a contradiction, but it underscores that no single authoritative loss figure exists yet.
**What happened:** Payment Processor supports meta-transactions via "trusted forwarder" contracts, which — per Limit Break's own design — anyone can deploy. By pairing a custom trusted-forwarder contract with specially crafted calldata, an attacker could make Payment Processor treat an arbitrary third-party wallet as the counterparty to a trade, without that wallet's owner ever signing anything. Two abuse paths were used: (1) for wallets that had left an NFT-transfer operator approval live, the attacker accepted a self-created zero-price offer "as" the victim, moving the NFT out for free; (2) for wallets that had approved WETH or another ERC-20 for making offers, the attacker listed a worthless NFT and forced the wallet to "buy" it, pulling the approved tokens instead.
**Root cause:** The trusted-forwarder mechanism let the contract accept a forged assertion of "who is on the other side of this trade" without binding it to a genuine signature from that party — a meta-transaction/counterparty-authentication bypass, compounded by the fact that long-dead marketplace integrations left blanket operator approvals live on user wallets for roughly two years with no expiry.
**Bug class:** access-control / meta-transaction-authorization (trusted-forwarder counterparty spoofing); secondary risk factor: unpausable, unupgradeable legacy contract holding live approvals indefinitely.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Meta-transaction "trusted forwarder" patterns — counterparty spoofing, not just signature replay**

The Magic Eden/Limit Break bug is a genuinely different shape than most of this series' recent incidents: it's not a stolen key, not an oracle, not a bridge proof — it's a marketplace/escrow contract trusting a *forwarder-asserted* counterparty identity instead of a genuine signature from that party. Any contract that lets third parties deploy their own "trusted forwarder" or relayer contracts to submit meta-transactions on behalf of others is worth auditing for whether it actually verifies a signature bound to the claimed party, or just trusts whatever identity the forwarder's calldata asserts.

**2. Unpausable legacy contracts still holding live blanket approvals**

Payment Processor V2 has no pause or upgrade path, so the bug is permanent — the only mitigation is users individually revoking old `setApprovalForAll`-style operator approvals, years after they stopped using the integration. If scope includes any NFT marketplace, lending, or DeFi contract that relies on long-lived operator/spend approvals, check both (a) whether the contract itself has an emergency pause, and (b) whether the project has any way to mass-invalidate stale approvals — the absence of either turns any future logic bug into an indefinite, unfixable liability for users who forgot they ever approved it.

**3. Bridge/rollup "trusted poster" functions — verification logic, not just the poster's key (still live, Payy)**

Payy's `verifyRollup` incident remains the clean counter-example to assume-it's-key-theft: the trusted poster address itself wasn't compromised, yet the function still paid out. The working theory — a forged burn message inside otherwise-valid proven public inputs — is a reminder to check whether "proof verification" actually constrains every field an attacker could manipulate, or only the ones the auditor thought to check.

**4. Cross-chain mint authorization tied to block/light-client validation (still live, Meter.io)**

No new detail has emerged, but the standing lesson holds: any bridge minting wrapped assets based on source-chain block or transaction proofs is worth checking for boundary/deserialization handling on externally supplied proof data.

**5. Chain-level governance authority overriding app-level admin/multisig controls (Cosmos/CosmWasm) — still unresolved, still no post-mortem**

Neutron's `MsgUpdateAdmin` privilege-escalation path is now five days old with no published technical report. For any Cosmos-SDK/CosmWasm protocol in scope, calculate the ratio between the cost of winning a governance vote at spot price right before tally and the value of assets that vote's outcome can control, and check for timelocks between proposal tally and execution.

**6. Client-side signing implementations in hardware/software wallets — treat early figures as a floor**

DCENT's XRP leg has grown from an initial ~$4.3M-equivalent estimate two weeks ago to ~12.4M XRP (~$20M) today, with the mechanism still undisclosed. Prioritize signing/key-derivation code review in any hardware or mobile wallet audit, and don't anchor on a project's first-reported loss number.
