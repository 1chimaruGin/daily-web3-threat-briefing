### CoinDCX — Exchange Confirms $16M Loss It Had Called a "False Alarm" Days Earlier (Day 6)

**Chain:** TRON (affected wallets/deposits), Ethereum (USDT blacklist action).
**Status:** Escalated/resolved-on-CoinDCX's-terms. Confirmed Oct 25, 2026.
**What happened:** On October 21-22, 2026, blockchain investigator "Darcy" flagged roughly $16 million (15.99 million USDT plus 5,783.81 TRX) moving out of a CoinDCX-linked wallet. CEO Sumit Gupta replied within hours that the wallets were "not breached," that they had been compromised in CoinDCX's July 2025 incident, and that "the hackers seem to be re-using the old wallet addresses and this sounds like a false alarm"; CoinDCX's official account called the claims "misleading." On October 25, CoinDCX reversed course in substance: in a blog post, it said unusual activity "had impacted our old, deprecated wallet infrastructure which had unknown vulnerabilities," that its automated sweeping service "had failed for a particular period," letting user deposits accumulate at an address the exchange no longer actively used, and that "this led to a loss of 15.99 million USDT which was absorbed by the company." CoinDCX frames this as an "operational loss," not a hack, and says current wallets and cold storage were unaffected. On-chain investigator ZachXBT disputes the framing: he says one of the "retired" addresses continued sweeping deposits into CoinDCX's live operational hot wallet as recently as April-May 2026, meaning the exposure was not confined to abandoned infrastructure as CoinDCX suggests, and has told CoinDCX users to withdraw funds. DefiLlama's hacks database lists the entry (dated Oct 21, 2026) under the category "Key Compromise / Hot Wallet Key Compromised" — a categorization that implicitly rejects CoinDCX's "not a breach" framing. Tether has separately blacklisted USDT wallets tied to the outflow, with an earlier report putting the frozen sum at roughly $2.2M.
**Root cause:** Disputed. CoinDCX's own account: an automated deposit-sweep service failure on a wallet address compromised in a prior (July 2025) breach, letting new deposits pile up unswept rather than a fresh intrusion. ZachXBT's account: that address was never fully retired from CoinDCX's active deposit-sweep pipeline, meaning the July 2025 key compromise effectively never stopped being exploitable.
**Bug class:** key-management / incomplete-wallet-retirement-after-breach — a wallet address compromised in a prior incident was not fully decommissioned from automated fund-sweep infrastructure, letting new user deposits accumulate at a still-exposed address for an extended period.

### Swell (swETH/rswETH) — Hot-Key Near-Miss Escalates to a Full Withdrawal Freeze (Day 6)

**Chain:** Ethereum.
**Status:** Escalated. Compromise first reported as of 14:25 UTC Oct 21, 2026; characterized Oct 22 as a contained near-miss ("all funds safe," no withdrawal impact); by Oct 24, Swell had frozen withdrawals for both affected tokens.
**What happened:** An unknown actor took control of Swell DAO's operational hot keys, including the oracle bot that sets swETH and rswETH exchange rates, and used that access to raise both tokens' rates at the maximum allowed pace (1% per hour) with no new ETH backing the increase. The initial Oct 22 reporting described the situation as contained: no ETH had been deposited into or withdrawn from affected contracts, four malicious upgrade contracts had been staged (capable of sweeping ~2,513 ETH, ~$6M, if executed through Swell's timelock) but none had fired, and the protocol remained "fully operational." That status changed by October 24: per Unchained (cited via a secondary aggregator), "Swell responded by freezing withdrawals for both tokens entirely," stating it intends to correct the exchange rates before allowing redemptions to resume. Reporting from Cryptopolitan claims the unbacked rate hikes followed exposure of a key connected to Railway, the cloud deployment platform already carrying its own standing security-disclosure entry in this briefing series — but this attribution is explicitly hedged even by the outlets reporting it ("Neither report detailed the exact mechanism by which the leaked key enabled rate manipulation"), and Swell itself has not confirmed a Railway link in its own statements found this window.
**Root cause:** A compromised DAO-operated hot key with combined price-oracle and (staged, unexecuted) upgrade authority; the claimed originating leak point (Railway) remains third-party-attributed, not confirmed by Swell.
**Bug class:** key-management / hot-key-and-oracle-compromise — unchanged bug class from the near-miss stage, but the real-world blast radius escalated this window from "staged risk, no impact" to "live, user-facing withdrawal freeze."

### NEM Token (BNB Chain) — PancakeSwap Pool Drain, New to This Series (Day 5)

**Chain:** BNB Chain.
**Status:** Closed, one-off incident. First disclosed October 22, 2026 by CertiK Alert; not previously covered in this briefing series.
**What happened:** CertiK Alert flagged an exploit against the NEM token's USDT/NEM liquidity pool on PancakeSwap on October 22, 2026, estimating losses at approximately $820,000; SlowMist corroborated and refined the figure to $821,920. The vulnerable code sits in a contract called NemTokenV2, centered on a mechanism named "pendingLpSellRecycle" designed to track and process sell pressure flowing into the liquidity pool. The contract made a basic accounting mistake: it treated any transfer of tokens into the liquidity pair as sell volume, without checking whether a real sale had actually occurred. The attacker exploited this by sending NEM into the pool and then skimming the resulting phantom surplus back out once the pool's real balance drifted above PancakeSwap's recorded reserves, repeating the loop 42 times per SlowMist while also artificially inflating NEM's on-chain price. Proceeds were bridged to Ethereum.
**Bug class:** accounting / fee-tracking-misclassification — a sell-volume tracker treated ordinary liquidity-pool-bound token transfers as sales, creating a skimmable phantom surplus with no real sale behind it.

## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

### Electrum Wallet — Fee-Bump Transaction Substitution, Patched in 4.8.3 (New)

A malicious Electrum server could intercept an unconfirmed outgoing Bitcoin payment, hide the legitimate broadcast transaction, and substitute a replacement with an attacker-controlled destination address chosen to match the real recipient's last four characters. Electrum did not verify the signature on the substitute while it sat unconfirmed. If the victim then tried to speed up the (actually-already-broadcast, now-hidden) payment via Replace-By-Fee, approving the fee bump meant unknowingly signing a valid transaction that paid the attacker instead. Two victims were confirmed (Oct 11 and Oct 18, 2026), the second losing funds specifically while attempting a fee increase; developers have not disclosed the number of malicious servers involved, the total amount stolen, or whether further victims exist. The fix, incorporated October 23 with downloadable binaries released October 24 (version 4.8.3), adds signature verification for unconfirmed transactions that spend inputs owned by the user's own wallet. Affects all Electrum versions prior to 4.8.3.
**Bug class:** signature-hygiene / unverified-unconfirmed-transaction-replacement — a wallet trusted an unconfirmed, unsigned-but-displayed transaction substitute from a third-party server instead of verifying its signature before allowing a user action (fee bump) to effectively re-authorize it.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. "Operational loss" as a euphemism for incomplete key rotation (CoinDCX, escalated this window)**

CoinDCX's own explanation — an automated sweep process kept routing funds through a wallet address it says was "retired" after a prior breach — describes a very specific and checkable failure mode: decommissioning a compromised address from the *label* used in incident reporting without decommissioning it from the *infrastructure* that still actively sweeps funds through it. For any exchange, custodian, or protocol that has rotated keys after a past incident: verify the old address is excluded from every automated sweep/consolidation job, not just from the "current hot wallet" documentation. ZachXBT's rebuttal (sweeps as recent as April-May 2026) shows this gap can persist for the better part of a year undetected.

**2. Hot keys with combined oracle + upgrade authority, revisited under real impact (Swell, escalated this window)**

Last window's lesson — isolate a price-oracle-setting key from upgrade/admin authority — now has a second data point: the compromise that was "contained" with no funds moved still forced a full withdrawal freeze once the inflated rate made normal redemptions unsafe. A circuit breaker that stops the *oracle* from drifting doesn't fully solve the problem if the same credential retains the power to stage fund-draining upgrades; audit whether a compromised rate-setting key can be rotated out *without* also needing to rotate upgrade authority, since the two may be more entangled than they appear from a near-miss alone.

**3. Sell-volume/fee-tracking logic on custom liquidity-pool tokens (NEM, new this window)**

NEM's bug is a narrow but recurring BNB Chain pattern (the article notes similar prior 2026 incidents on Movie Token, ATM, and LULA): a custom tokenomics contract tracks "sells into the pool" by watching for transfers to the LP pair address, rather than verifying an actual swap occurred. Any token with anti-whale, tax, or auto-liquidity mechanisms keyed off "transfer-to-pair" as a proxy for "sale" is worth checking for this exact skim-and-repeat pattern.

**4. Trusting unconfirmed transaction history from an untrusted peer (Electrum, new this window)**

The Electrum flaw is a reminder that "unconfirmed" and "unverified" are not the same risk category: a wallet that displays a server-supplied unconfirmed transaction as fact, and lets a user action (fee bump) implicitly re-sign over it, is vulnerable regardless of how the UI frames the action. Worth checking any wallet client (hardware or software) that accepts RBF, CPFP, or similar fee-adjustment flows for whether it independently verifies the transaction being modified actually matches what the user most recently broadcast.

**5. Governance-takeover financing and incident-response credibility — still the dominant theme (standing, reinforced)**

X100's and Arkadiko's flash-loan/spot-buy Senate-seat takeovers remain the two clearest recent cases of "no snapshot, no timelock" governance; both are still unrecovered. Meanwhile this window's two escalations (CoinDCX, Swell) are both cases where the first public response *understated* the eventual outcome — "false alarm" became a confirmed $16M loss, and "fully operational, no impact" became a full withdrawal freeze. Read the on-chain trail (Bitquery, PeckShield, Blockaid, Chainalysis, ZachXBT, DefiLlama), not a project's first-day statement, before treating any standing incident as resolved.
