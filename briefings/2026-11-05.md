### CoreVault — Flash-Loan Oracle Manipulation Drains InsurancePool (~$112,200, BNB Smart Chain)

**Chain:** BNB Smart Chain (BSC).
**Status:** Confirmed loss; no post-mortem or recovery announcement published as of writing.
**Impact:** CertiK's security dashboard valued the haul at approximately **$112,200** (separately cited as ~$112,300 by other trackers, a gap the reporting attributes to BNB price-timing variance rather than a disputed fact); the stable, chain-level figure is **160.26 BNB**, all drained from CoreVault's dedicated "InsurancePool" backstop fund.
**What happened:** On Nov 4, 2026, an attacker used a flash loan to manipulate CoreVault's price oracle — described in reporting only generically as a feed reliant on "a source a large trade can move, such as a thin liquidity pool," with no specific oracle provider or exact mechanism (e.g., spot-price fallback vs. stale price) confirmed in available sourcing. The manipulated price let the attacker's redemption be calculated at roughly **33 times** its legitimate value, paying out far more than the attacker's actual position should have allowed, draining the InsurancePool before the flash loan was repaid in the same transaction. The attack was flagged by on-chain monitor TenArmorAlert and valued by CertiK.
**Root cause:** Flash-loan-funded price-oracle manipulation enabling an inflated redemption calculation — the specific oracle design flaw (which price source, whether a circuit breaker or sanity bound existed and was bypassed) is not established in available reporting.
**Remediation:** None reported. No team statement, post-mortem, or recovery effort has been published as of writing.
**Attribution:** None.
**Bug class:** logic-error / flash-loan-funded oracle manipulation inflating a redemption/payout calculation, draining a protocol's insurance/backstop reserve rather than user deposits directly.

### Binance — Laundering Enters THORChain SOL-to-BTC Phase (Day 7)

**Chain:** Ethereum, Solana, Bitcoin (via THORChain swap route); recovery figures otherwise unchanged across the full multi-chain footprint.
**Status:** Recovery essentially complete per Binance's own figures (unchanged this window); laundering of the stolen funds is now active and moving into a new phase.
**Impact:** Binance's own figures are unchanged from the prior window: approximately **98% of affected tokens replenished**, approximately **$2.85 billion frozen/intercepted**. The total theft figure remains cited inconsistently across sources in the **$6.3-6.4 billion** range, not reconciled to one number by this series.
**What happened:** Per Lookonchain and CoinDesk (citing the FBI's Oct 31 TraderTraitor attribution alert, which urged crypto service providers including THORNode operators to block linked transactions), the attackers resumed laundering activity this window by converting stolen Solana into Bitcoin: SOL was split across multiple wallets, sold for USDC, bridged to Ethereum, converted to ETH, and then swapped to BTC via THORChain. Lookonchain reported the attackers had moved roughly **486,970 SOL (~$46.4 million)** through this route, with the hackers having moved approximately **81% of the total stolen SOL** as of this window. A separate on-chain analysis (SomaXBT, dated Nov 1) found approximately **$48.3 million** swapped via THORChain with **$42.7 million** actually received net of fees/slippage (~12% lost), across roughly 2,697 deposits. THORChain's native token RUNE rose **10.6% to $0.637** on the increased swap volume. THORChain itself had briefly halted on Oct 31 after an unrelated Ethereum block reorg exposed a refund fault that erroneously paid out 10 ETH — a separate, smaller issue from the laundering activity, now resolved per secondary reporting.
**Root cause:** Unchanged from prior disclosure (EIP-7702 delegation abuse following a compromised signing-pipeline credential; see Nov 3-4 editions of this series for the full chain).
**Remediation:** Unchanged; Binance states it has rebuilt the wallet system and revoked related credentials.
**Attribution:** Unchanged — FBI/TRM Labs/Chainalysis maintain North Korea/TraderTraitor attribution; North Korea continues to deny involvement (no new statement found this window).
**Bug class:** Unchanged (key-management / EIP-7702 delegation persistence mechanism); this window's development is purely in the laundering/fund-movement phase, not a new technical disclosure.

### Mercado Bitcoin — Phased Reopening Announced for Nov 9; Attacker Sells Altcoins for ETH (Day 4)

**Chain:** Multi-chain exchange hot-wallet infrastructure, spanning up to 17 blockchains per Chainalysis's prior count.
**Status:** Active investigation continues; deposits/withdrawals remain suspended; a phased recovery timeline has now been published.
**Impact:** Loss estimates remain fragmented across trackers in the roughly **$38-49 million** range, unreconciled, as reported in the prior edition. New this window: Arkham reported the attacker sold approximately **$10.55-11.45 million** (figures vary slightly by snapshot/source) in stolen altcoins for ETH.
**What happened:** Mercado Bitcoin announced on Nov 5 that the platform will begin a phased recovery starting **Nov 9**: the first phase restores **view-only** access (balances, statements, holdings, investment details), with trading, withdrawal, and transfer functions to be restored only after each module passes final security verification — no date given for those later phases. Separately, Arkham monitoring found the attacker converting stolen tokens (a reported mix including PEPE, AAVE, PENDLE, EVA, VIRTUAL, UNI, ONDO and others, more than 136 distinct tokens total) into **4,605 ETH across 338 swaps** via MetaMask Router over roughly two days. A bridging attempt of roughly **$650,000** toward Bitcoin appears to have stalled, per Arkham, described as a failed attempt rather than a completed move.

**Remediation:** Mercado Bitcoin reiterated its guarantee that "no customer will lose their assets," stating balances remain recorded and it will replace affected assets with company funds, backed by a cash position bolstered by a prior $20 million Tether investment.
**Attribution:** None established. No confirmed link to the Binance hack or to the BitMart hack despite the latter's 53-minutes-prior timing (see Section 1).
**Bug class:** Unchanged (key-management / transaction-authorization-signing-credential compromise).

### $133M+ Multi-Exchange Stablecoin Theft — Victim Named: Crédit Mutuel Arkéa Confirms Attack, Disputes Fortuneo as Entry Point (Day 5)

**Chain:** Ethereum (USDC/USDT withdrawals, converted via UniswapX into ETH); downstream laundering continuing toward Monero.
**Status:** Active tracing; victim now named (previously anonymous "EU entity"); the bank confirms it was attacked but disputes a key detail of how.
**Impact:** Unchanged headline figure — ZachXBT's estimate remains **"more than $133 million,"** with Bitquery's narrower, independently-traced figure of **$108.8 million in USDC** across Kraken, OKX and Crypto.com over roughly 21 hours on Nov 1-2 standing as the more rigorously documented subset. As of the last snapshot, roughly **$78.3 million** remained in attacker-linked Ethereum addresses, with only **~$3.74 million** still freezable in USDC.
**What happened:** On Nov 4, onchain investigator ZachXBT named French cooperative banking group **Crédit Mutuel Arkéa** (whose ~40 subsidiaries include online bank Fortuneo) as the suspected victim. **Crédit Mutuel Arkéa confirmed to CoinDesk on Nov 4** that it had suffered an attack that "enabled fraudulent transactions" but said the situation was "now under control," and a bank spokesperson stated: "This incident has no financial consequences for [our clients]. Their interests are fully protected." The bank did not disclose a dollar figure. Separately, French crypto journalist Grégory Raymond (co-founder of Big Whale) specifically named **Fortuneo** as the target — a claim **Crédit Mutuel Arkéa formally denied**, stating that no "entry" was made through Fortuneo and that "the fraudsters used an account of the group itself and not customer accounts" from Fortuneo, Crédit Mutuel de Bretagne, or Crédit Mutuel du Sud-Ouest. The mechanism as now described: attackers used fabricated identities that passed KYC checks to open accounts at Kraken and other exchanges, then moved funds from an Arkéa group-level account to those exchange accounts under recipient names matching the account holders (evading simple name-mismatch fraud checks), before converting to stablecoins and withdrawing to external wallets. Bitquery's structural finding from the prior window — that 11 of 15 Ethereum destination addresses had prior legitimate payment history from the same exchanges — remains consistent with this now-clearer picture of established, name-matched withdrawal destinations rather than fresh attacker wallets.
**Root cause:** Now substantially clearer but still not fully resolved: a dispute exists between the named bank (which says the compromise was at the group-account level, not through Fortuneo specifically) and a journalist's sourcing (naming Fortuneo as the entry point) — this series treats the entry-point mechanism as disputed pending further disclosure, while treating the fact of an attack on the Arkéa group as confirmed by the bank itself.
**Remediation:** Crédit Mutuel Arkéa states it is working with judicial and police authorities; no further technical remediation detail disclosed.
**Attribution:** Unchanged — ZachXBT maintains "the laundering behavior does not resemble patterns typically associated with North Korea's Lazarus Group," a direct distinction from the Binance incident's attribution.
**Bug class:** Newly characterized this window: social-engineering / fabricated-identity KYC-passing account creation at centralized exchanges, used as a laundering destination for funds moved from a financial institution's own account (not customer accounts) — a materially different bug class than a simple "exchange login credential theft," now that the victim and a disputed mechanism are both named.

### BTCPay Server — Hot-Wallet-Theft Facts Unchanged; Separate v2.4.7 Release Patches 85 Security Findings (Day 5)

**Chain:** Bitcoin and Litecoin.
**Status:** Hot-wallet-theft investigation unchanged from the prior window (~$1.63M traced, operators on affected versions still told to migrate to a fresh server). Separately, a general security-hardening release shipped.
**Impact:** No change to the ~$1.63 million theft figure. Separately, **BTCPay Server 2.4.7**, released **Oct 29, 2026** (reported on this window via secondary coverage), patches **85 security findings**: 1 critical, 13 high, 52 medium, and 19 low severity.
**What happened:** Two independent secondary sources (TokenPost, NewsBTC) confirm the 85-finding breakdown for 2.4.7 and explicitly state this release is **unconnected** to the hot-wallet theft reported in the prior window — the theft exploited a different, already-identified vulnerability class fixed in version 2.4.2 (shipped Aug 7), while 2.4.7 addresses a separate, broader set of findings. Note: one lower-authority aggregator source (Cryptometer) attributed the 85 findings' discovery to an "Anthropic OSS Scanner program" — this series checked two other sources describing the same release and found **no corroboration** for that attribution; it does not appear in BTCPay's own framing as reported elsewhere and is treated here as unverified and likely inaccurate, not repeated as fact.
**Root cause:** Theft: unchanged, administrator-access compromise, exact vector still unconfirmed. 2.4.7: general vulnerability-review findings, no single root cause (85 distinct findings across severities).
**Remediation:** Unchanged for the theft (migrate to fresh server, don't just update). For 2.4.7: administrators urged to update promptly given the critical-severity finding included.
**Bug class:** Theft: unchanged (key-management / administrator-access compromise). 2.4.7: general self-hosted-software hardening release, not a single bug class.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Flash-loan-funded oracle manipulation against redemption/payout logic backed by thin liquidity (CoreVault, this window)**

CoreVault's InsurancePool drain is a textbook reminder that "we have an oracle" is not the same as "our oracle can't be moved by a loan the size of our own TVL." For any audit touching a redemption, liquidation, or insurance-payout calculation: confirm what the price feed's worst-case manipulability actually is relative to realistic flash-loan sizes (not relative to the protocol's own liquidity, which the attacker doesn't need to use), check whether a single-block price input feeds directly into a payout multiplier with no sanity bound, and verify whether any "backstop" or "insurance" pool is reachable by the same price path as the primary collateral — a separate pool with shared price-dependency is not actually a separate risk bucket.

**2. Shared-attacker timing correlation across same-week exchange breaches (BitMart → Mercado Bitcoin, this window)**

BitMart's founder flagged a 53-minute gap between attacks on BitMart and Mercado Bitcoin as evidence of a shared actor — a reminder that incident response and bounty triage should actively check timing adjacency against other concurrent disclosures, not treat each breach as isolated. If you're reviewing exchange-adjacent infrastructure (custody, withdrawal-authorization services, KYC/onboarding) this week, assume an active campaign against multiple targets using a reusable technique, not a single opportunistic actor.

**3. KYC-passing fabricated-identity accounts as a laundering/fraud destination, not just a compliance gap (Crédit Mutuel Arkéa/Fortuneo, this window)**

The mechanism now described for the EU theft — fabricated identities that pass KYC, opening exchange accounts that then receive transfers under a name matching the account holder — defeats a specific, common fraud control (destination-name matching) without defeating KYC itself. For bounty/audit scope that includes exchange-side withdrawal controls: the relevant question isn't just "is KYC enforced" but "what happens when the attacker's KYC-verified identity and the sending account's registered name are made to match by design." This is an operational/fraud-control gap more than a smart-contract bug, but it's directly relevant to any bridge, exchange, or payment-rail scope that relies on name-matching as an anti-fraud control.

**4. Cross-chain laundering rails under active regulatory pressure (THORChain, Binance hack, this window)**

The FBI's Oct 31 alert specifically named THORNode operators as a channel to block — and the attackers used it anyway, converting SOL to ETH to BTC within days of that alert. For any protocol whose design intentionally resists address-level blocking (no admin pause, no blacklist), expect continued use as a laundering rail regardless of law-enforcement pressure; this is a governance/compliance question for protocol teams more than a bug-bounty target, but it shapes where recovered-funds narratives will and won't apply.

**5. Distinguish "unrelated vulnerability, same product" disclosures from the incident they're adjacent to (BTCPay Server 2.4.7, this window)**

Two sources independently confirmed BTCPay's 2.4.7 patch (85 findings) is unconnected to the still-unresolved hot-wallet-theft incident reported the prior window — but a lower-quality aggregator conflated them with an unverifiable "Anthropic OSS Scanner" attribution. When a vendor ships a general hardening release during an active incident, don't assume correlation; verify root-cause-level distinctness against the vendor's own release notes before treating two disclosures as one story — and don't repeat unverifiable third-party attributions about discovery methods.
