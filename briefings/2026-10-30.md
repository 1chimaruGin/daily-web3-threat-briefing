### Binance — Suspected Multi-Billion-Dollar Hot-Wallet Hack via EIP-7702 Delegation (Loss Figure Disputed, Mechanism Unconfirmed)

**Chain:** Multi-chain. Confirmed outflows on Ethereum (the primary leg, ~$3.5B+ into one address within 40 minutes), Bitcoin (~18,270 BTC, ~$1.44B), and Tron (~$630M), with Aave and Arbitrum Security Council actions confirming related exposure on Arbitrum and Avalanche; secondary reporting (not independently verified here) adds smaller amounts on XRP Ledger, Solana, Optimism, and Base.
**Status:** Active investigation. Binance has not officially confirmed this as a hack in its own language ("we have not confirmed any impact to user funds") even as co-founder Changpeng Zhao and co-CEO Yi He both publicly treated it as one. Deposits, withdrawals, and Binance Alpha trading are suspended with no restoration timeline. No post-mortem published.
**Impact:** Disputed and still moving. The lower-bound on-chain tally (hot-wallet outflow only) is roughly $2.8B; Decrypt's cross-chain tally put it at "at least $5.57 billion"; Arkham Intelligence's wallet-cluster label "binance-hacker-1029" held roughly $5.84B in confirmed holdings (explicitly not the same as a confirmed theft total); Chainalysis's estimate — the most specific and recency-dated figure found — is $5.912 billion "at the time of the incident," which outlets describe as nearly four times the ~$1.5B Bybit hack and potentially the largest crypto theft in history if it holds. Binance's own "verified frozen" total, which the company says is distinct from and smaller than the total theft, rose from $2.54B at 23:22 UTC Oct 29 to approximately $3B by 00:52 UTC Oct 30 — Binance explicitly said it was "clarifying differing figures circulating publicly, including references to $1.8 billion." None of these figures are reconciled with each other.
**What happened:** At approximately 20:21 UTC on October 29, an address funded via Tornado Cash submitted a transaction installing an EIP-7702 delegation on a Binance hot wallet, bundling 50 separate authorization attempts — one of which matched the wallet's required nonce and was accepted. EIP-7702 lets an ordinary externally-owned account temporarily delegate its code execution to a smart contract; here, that delegation handed execution authority to an unverified, attacker-controlled "sweeper" contract. At 20:24 UTC, the compromised wallet transferred 167,464 ETH, 1.615 billion USDT, and more than 60 other tokens in a single sweep. On-chain researcher "Spreek" was among the first to flag the transfer publicly; Binance acknowledged "a potential security issue" shortly after via its official channels. The attacker subsequently deposited roughly 60 million USDT onto Aave and borrowed approximately 19,762 ETH (~$44.6M) against it — stablecoins can be frozen by issuers, ETH cannot — prompting Aave to freeze 175 reserves across its V3 deployment (16 networks) and 117 reserves across V4 (4 networks) as a precaution roughly 40 minutes later, while stating its own contracts were unaffected and all positions remained overcollateralized. Kamino, a Solana DeFi protocol, separately paused its Solana and Bitcoin markets as a precaution. The Arbitrum Security Council took an emergency on-chain action to freeze approximately $30.5 million (74.56 million ARB plus 8,425 ETH) tied to the attacker's wallet, rendering it immovable except by further Security Council or Arbitrum DAO action.
**Root cause:** Unconfirmed and actively disputed. Multiple sources agree EIP-7702 itself was not the vulnerability — it was the mechanism used to execute the sweep, not the flaw that let the attacker gain control. How the attacker obtained the signature/authorization needed to install the delegation in the first place is the open question. Blockaid co-founder and CEO Ido Ben-Natan's preliminary assessment favors "key misuse" — a compromise of Binance's signing infrastructure that tricked or coerced an authorized signing system into approving the malicious transaction — over a leaked private key: "We don't think a key was leaked... it seems to be a compromise of signing infrastructure." A separate, less-corroborated theory (via SlowMist's Cosine, reported secondhand) suggests the hot wallet's automated signing system may have been processing routine withdrawals and "blindly" signed a batch of transactions that happened to include the malicious one. Neither explanation is confirmed. Attribution to North Korea's Lazarus Group is circulating among independent analysts citing signing-path similarities to the Bybit and Bitget hacks, but no firm or government agency has made an official attribution, and researchers explicitly caution this scale and method has not previously been seen even in Lazarus-attributed cases.
**Bug class:** key-management / signing-infrastructure-compromise enabling an EIP-7702 delegation takeover of a hot wallet's execution authority — the precise entry point (leaked key vs. compromised signer vs. tricked approval) is unresolved.

### Polymarket — $2.19M-$2.65M Drained via Compromised Third-Party Monitoring Vendor (DebugBear)

**Chain:** Polygon (frontend/session-layer compromise; Polymarket's own smart contracts confirmed unaffected).
**Status:** Resolved same day. Trading paused and fully restored within roughly an hour; full reimbursement pledged.
**Impact:** Disputed. Crypto-economy.com cites $2.65 million drained across 710+ wallets, sourced to Polygonscan and Arkham Intelligence. Security firm Hudson Rock explicitly declines to confirm any aggregate total, noting only that roughly $2.19 million was visible at one hardcoded fallback-recipient address and stating that "the number of affected users, aggregate losses attributable to this incident... remain unresolved."
**What happened:** Around 18:00 UTC on October 29, a malicious script began being served from the legitimate CDN of DebugBear, a third-party website-performance/real-user-monitoring (RUM) vendor integrated into Polymarket's frontend. Hudson Rock independently downloaded the script twice and confirmed an identical 15,187-byte payload. The script read Polymarket's own authentication data out of browser localStorage, traversed React's internal component tree to locate the live trading client and signer, checked a user's portfolio value against a threshold, and then — for standard wallet (EOA) accounts — batch-transferred ERC-1155 position tokens, or — for Magic/non-EOA accounts — placed aggressive sell orders and then transferred the proceeds, all via EIP-712-signed batches routed through Polymarket's own relayer infrastructure. It reported telemetry back to a DebugBear subdomain. By 18:05 UTC Polymarket had put its matching engine into cancel-only mode; a code commit removing the DebugBear script is timestamped 17:59:20 (Hudson Rock explicitly flags this as a build timestamp, not a verified containment time); Polymarket issued a reimbursement guarantee at 18:33 UTC and declared the incident resolved with trading restored by 19:05 UTC. Polymarket stated via X: "One of our third-party vendors was compromised, and we have referred this matter to the relevant law enforcement authorities," and separately, "We'll make this right and are fully reimbursing all affected traders."
**Root cause:** Supply-chain compromise of a third-party vendor's script, not a flaw in Polymarket's own code. How DebugBear's CDN itself was compromised is unresolved — no verified statement from DebugBear was found, and Hudson Rock's report leaves the initial-access question explicitly open.
**Bug class:** supply-chain / third-party-monitoring-vendor-script-compromise enabling session hijacking and unauthorized fund transfer — structurally the same pattern as the Udesk chat-widget compromise tracked in this series two days ago, now recurring against a different vendor type (performance monitoring rather than customer-service chat).

### ChainPort Bridge / Iron Fish — 2.98B wIRON Over-Minted on Base (38.7x Real Supply), Root Cause Undisclosed

**Chain:** Base (minting side); Iron Fish's own L1 (native IRON, the asset being wrapped).
**Status:** Active/unresolved. Iron Fish has requested an emergency review from ChainPort and warned users off the bridge; no ChainPort statement found; no root-cause explanation published.
**Impact:** 2.98 billion wIRON minted against a real IRON supply the mint represents 38.7 times over; attacker-linked wallets realized a combined ~$197.4K selling into thin liquidity; a further 192.0 million wIRON was burned by 212 separate senders attempting to redeem real IRON, with redemption status unconfirmed on-chain.
**What happened:** On October 27, the ChainPort bridge minted 2.98 billion wIRON — the Base-network wrapped representation of Iron Fish's native IRON token — across 14 separate mint transactions, each of which passed the bridge's own validation checks. Two newly created wallets then sold the entire minted amount, realizing a combined $197.4K; one routed its proceeds to FixedFloat (a no-KYC exchange), the other swapped its share for 0.865 BTC via THORChain, which moved again the following Wednesday morning. Separately, 212 senders burned a combined 192.0 million wIRON attempting to claim real IRON through the bridge's redemption path; whether ChainPort has honored or will honor those claims is not visible on-chain. ## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

### Ethereum Validator Clients Teku and Besu Patch Certificate Flaw That Could Redirect Block-Building Fees

Researchers discovered on October 17 that insufficient randomness had been used to generate private keys for eight intermediate certificate authorities operated by eMudhra, meaning those private keys could be mathematically recovered from information available in the publicly issued certificates themselves. The risk applies specifically to Ethereum validators that rely on external services — a remote signer, a separate beacon node, or an MEV block-building relay — authenticated via TLS certificates chained to one of the affected eMudhra CAs: an attacker positioned between a validator and such a service could forge a certificate, impersonate the service, and trick the validator into proposing a block that pays fees to the attacker's address instead of the validator's own. Default mainnet validator setups that do not rely on external signing/beacon infrastructure are not affected, and no exploitation has been reported. The Ethereum client Teku released version 26.10.3, and Besu released version 26.10.2, both removing trust in eMudhra-chained certificates; Docker users should pull the updated images, while operators running the software directly need to update their Java runtime separately (moving off the now-unsupported Java 26 to Java 25, per Besu's guidance).

### EIP-7702 Delegation Risk Remains a Live, Pre-Existing Pattern Beyond the Binance Incident

Independent of the Binance hack above, EIP-7702 wallet-delegation abuse has been a recurring, lower-severity theft pattern through October: researchers studying early EIP-7702 authorization transactions found attacker-controlled contracts tied to the majority of them (one study cites 63%), and individual victims have lost five- and six-figure sums to delegation phishing this month (e.g., a reported $22.7K loss on October 14). The structural issue researchers point to is a wallet-UX gap: many wallets present a delegation authorization as an unreadable hex blob rather than a human-readable warning that the user is handing a contract permanent control over their account's code execution. This is the same primitive — not the same root cause — implicated in the much larger Binance incident, and is worth tracking separately as its own standing risk to retail wallets.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Signing-infrastructure trust boundaries around EIP-7702 delegation (Binance, new and dominant this window)**

Whatever let the Binance attacker install a delegation authorization on a hot wallet — a leaked key, a tricked approval system, or a deeper signing-infrastructure compromise — the underlying primitive is now proven at the largest possible scale: an EIP-7702 delegation transaction can hand total execution control of a wallet to an attacker-controlled contract in a single confirmed transaction, and a hot-wallet operator's automated transaction-approval pipeline is a legitimate, high-value attack surface distinct from the private key itself. For any exchange, custodian, or protocol operating hot wallets with automated signing, review: whether the signing system validates *what* a transaction authorizes (a delegation to an unverified contract) as rigorously as it validates *who* is requesting the signature; whether batched/bundled authorization transactions (like the reported 50-authorization bundle here) get the same scrutiny as a single one; and whether EIP-7702 delegation designators on deposit addresses are screened before crediting funds, since a delegated account is no longer behaving as a plain EOA.

**2. Third-party frontend/widget vendors as a session- and fund-hijacking surface — now the third time in a week (Polymarket/DebugBear, new; following Udesk/SlowMist and the Predict.fun documentation-site gap, both standing)**

This is the third incident this series has tracked in roughly a week where a third-party web component — a documentation site, a customer-service chat widget, now a website-performance monitoring script — sat in a position to read authenticated session data or hijack what a user sees, copies, or signs. The pattern is now common enough to treat as its own standing bug class rather than a one-off: any platform that embeds third-party JS on pages where users hold authenticated sessions or view/copy financial data should be checked for script pinning or subresource integrity, whether the vendor's script runs with unrestricted access to the host page's DOM and JS framework internals (Polymarket's React internals were directly walkable by the injected script), and whether a vendor compromise is detectable from the platform side before a user's funds move, not just after.

**3. DAO governance takeovers via low-activity, last-minute, privacy-mixer-funded voting remain a recurring, cheap attack (Hifi Finance, new; joining X100, Yapeswap, Arkadiko, all standing)**

Four votes was reportedly enough to seize Hifi Finance's governance. Across this series, governance takeovers now outnumber almost any other single bug class, and the common thread is thin, low-quorum voting activity that a well-timed, Tornado-Cash-funded voting position can overwhelm in the vote's final window. For any protocol with on-chain DAO governance controlling minting, treasury, or privileged parameters, check actual historical quorum/participation levels against the token supply required to flip a close vote in its final blocks — "the vote passed" is not the same as "the vote was contested by enough real participants to resist a late capital injection."

**4. Bridge mint-validation checks that pass despite gross oversupply (ChainPort/Iron Fish, new)**

A bridge minted 38.7 times the real supply of the asset it was wrapping, across 14 separate transactions, and its own validation checks did not catch it. Whatever the specific bypass — a missing supply-cap check, a stale oracle feed for backing reserves, a per-transaction limit that doesn't aggregate across repeated calls — this is a textbook bridge-accounting bug class already tracked in this series (WAX's negative-fee exploit is a cousin) and worth sweeping for on any bridge contract: does a wrapped-asset mint function check cumulative issuance against actual locked/bridged backing, or only against a per-call limit that a attacker can simply call repeatedly?

**5. Signing-certificate and key-generation entropy as an underexamined validator/infrastructure surface (Teku/Besu eMudhra flaw, new advisory)**

A certificate authority used by Ethereum validator infrastructure turned out to have generated private keys with insufficient randomness — recoverable from public certificate data alone. This is conceptually the same class of bug as the standing Qubic weak-seed-generation incident, just one layer up the stack (infrastructure/TLS rather than wallet-seed), and it's a reminder to check entropy sources not just in wallet and key-generation code under direct audit, but in any third-party certificate, TLS, or signing-infrastructure dependency a validator, custodian, or signing service relies on.
