## 1. 🔴 Active exploits (last 24h)

### SpiralCom — Uniswap V4 Spot-Price Collateral Manipulation (Sep 14)

**Chain:** Ethereum (Uniswap V4)
**Status:** Confirmed, closed. Small single-block loss.
**Impact:** ~10.7 ETH — recorded by SlowMist's incident database as **~$26,800**.

An attacker pushed the spot price of a Uniswap V4 pool inside a single Ethereum block and borrowed against the inflated collateral valuation. Reporting attributes the initial flag to the SlowMist team on X.

**Root cause (as reported):** SpiralCom's `SpiralHookV2` borrow path valued collateral from the live pool state (`poolManager.getSlot0()`) with no TWAP reference and no bound on how far the spot price could move before it was trusted. The protocol's same-block swap guard was keyed on `tx.origin`, so the attacker split the operation across multiple externally owned accounts to sidestep it and complete the pump-and-borrow atomically within one block.

> **Unconfirmed:** the specific count of EOAs used (reported as six) and the exact `SpiralHookV2.borrow()` / `noSameBlockSwap` symbol names come from secondary reporting summarising a SlowMist X post. No transaction hashes or attacker addresses were published in the sources reviewed. Treat the function names as indicative, not verified against deployed bytecode.

**Bug class:** price-manipulation (spot-price oracle, no TWAP), access-control bypass (`tx.origin`-keyed guard)

### Long (long.supply) — Bridge Keeper Fed Forged Withdrawal Events (Sep 14)

**Chain:** Robinhood Chain (destination) ← Arc (source)
**Status:** Confirmed. No contract bug, no key compromise.
**Impact:** **46.7928 WETH, ~$118,000.**

Long's custodial bridge released WETH from its Robinhood Chain vault after its off-chain keeper read *fabricated* Arc withdrawal/burn events. The keeper was reading burn events from a third-party public RPC because the official mainnet endpoint was not yet live. After the backup endpoint was saturated, the primary endpoint returned forged withdrawal logs inside a short window, and the keeper paid out against them.

**Root cause:** the keeper's safety checks covered replay and double-payment but never authenticated the *provenance* of the event data. There was no light-client proof, no multi-RPC quorum, and no cross-check of the claimed burn against an independent view of Arc. This is a trusted-data-source failure sitting entirely outside the audited contract surface — SlowMist classes it as a supply-chain attack.

**Bug class:** data-source-trust / oracle-input forgery (off-chain relayer), supply-chain

**Sources:**
- [SlowMist Hacked (incident database)](https://hacked.slowmist.io/)
- [OKX Orbit — analysis of the Long bridge keeper RPC failure](https://www.okx.com/en-us/orbit/insight/87440009188160)

## 2. 🟠 Ongoing — carried forward

### Liquid Network / Blockstream — $320M Elements Range-Proof Cache Collision (Day 9)

**Chain:** Liquid (Bitcoin sidechain)
**Status:** PARTIAL RECOVERY / PEG-OUTS FROZEN (Day 9, counting from disclosure on Sep 6).
**Impact:** ~3,996 BTC (~$320M) pegged out against ~4,000 unbacked L-BTC. **3,400 BTC returned Sep 7; ~598.5 BTC (~$47M) still held by the attacker.**

**Status as of the Sep 13–14 window:** unchanged from Sep 11. Block production and ordinary transactions resumed Sep 10; **PAK-authorised peg-outs remain suspended** while the BTC/L-BTC reserve is restored. On Sep 11 Blockstream publicly refused the attacker's self-declared 10% "bounty" demand — *"We will not pay for the return of stolen property. We will not abandon our users."* — and said it would pursue law enforcement and asset recovery instead. No return of the remaining ~598.5 BTC had occurred by Sep 14.

**Root cause:** Elements built its range-proof verification cache key by concatenating four values — the proof, the hidden amount, the hidden asset and the output script — **without length prefixes or delimiters**. Two different tuples could therefore serialise to the same key bytes, letting a crafted output reuse a cached "valid" verdict and mint unbacked L-BTC. The fix length-prefixes each field before hashing. Elements **v23.3.4** (released Sep 9) carries both the initial backport (Sep 3, 23.x line) and the hardening commit (merged Sep 8).

> **Attribution:** the actor self-describes as a white hat in on-chain OP_RETURN messages. Blockstream has **not** endorsed that characterisation and is treating the remaining coins as stolen property. Treat "white hat" as the attacker's claim, not a confirmed finding.

> **Disputed detail:** the mint transaction timestamp differs between sources — Liquid's own incident report gives 2026-09-06 15:53:10 UTC (block 4,050,336); Bitquery's reconstruction gives 13:53 UTC for the mint and 14:28 UTC for the federation peg-out. Unresolved.

**Bug class:** cache-key collision / ambiguous serialisation in consensus-critical validation

### Chainflip — TRON USDT Memo Double-Payout (Day 3)

**Chain:** TRON (Chainflip vault)
**Status:** TRON swaps still suspended as of Sep 14; rest of network recovery announced later (Sep 16).
**Impact:** **736,442.17 USDT**, drained between 01:44 and 03:10 UTC on Sep 12 via six liquidity-provider withdrawals paid twice.

**Root cause:** the attacker took a transaction Chainflip's validators had **already signed** and appended a malformed memo. Chainflip's TRON monitoring logic read the altered memo as a *failed swap* and issued a refund on top of the withdrawal that had already been paid. The patch restricts which TRON transfers may carry swap instructions.

**Status in this window:** as of Sep 13–14 the protocol had paused network-wide swaps and had not yet published its restart. The plan to reset TRON USDT provider balances to zero (recording pre-migration amounts separately on-chain) and the resumption of non-TRON swaps were announced on Sep 16, i.e. **after** this window. No funding source or payout schedule for affected LPs had been disclosed.

**Bug class:** protocol-logic (signed-payload malleability — memo not covered by the signature/settlement identity)

**Sources:**
- [CryptoSlate — Chainflip to reset TRON USDT provider balances to zero following $736,000 exploit](https://cryptoslate.com/chainflip-to-reset-tron-usdt-provider-balances-to-zero-following-736000-exploit/)

### Vesu / Pragma — Oracle Publishing Fault: recovery declared complete (Sep 13)

**Chain:** Starknet
**Status:** RESOLVED (asset recovery closed Sep 13; incident originated Sep 4).
**Impact:** 47 liquidations across 7 pools affecting 42 borrowers; ~$3M of collateral liquidated, estimated **$783,905** in borrower equity loss.

**The Sep 13 development:** Vesu confirmed **completion of asset recovery**, reporting **95% recovery at Sep 11 prices (93% at prices on the morning of the incident)**. Recovery was achieved by Vesu and Starknet Security Councils, curators and partners working with liquidators who **voluntarily returned funds**; some liquidators could not be identified or reached. Curators now distribute the recovered funds.

> Note the distinction: this is an *asset recovery milestone*, not confirmation that every user refund has landed. Pragma explicitly warns that neither it nor curators will contact users unsolicited about refunds — a standing phishing lure.

**Root cause (Pragma post-mortem):** not an attack. A stale conversion route in the asset registry mapped USDC to an inactive legacy bridge, producing a bad $3.07 USDT/USD reading. Freshness filtering then dropped three healthy observations outside the 60-minute window, leaving only the bad reading and one legitimate source — whose median came out at $2.036576, roughly 2× true. Both active publishers used the same SDK, so BTC, ETH, STRK and USDC prices were all divided by that inflated reference and appeared to halve. Feeds displayed a healthy-looking source count while all of them depended on the same two-source conversion input.

**Bug class:** oracle-failure (stale reference route + median-of-two after freshness filtering + hidden shared dependency)

**Sources:**

**No new CVEs affecting smart-contract or wallet tooling were disclosed in the Sep 13–14 window** in the sources reviewed.

### rekt.news publishes the Tectonic / Cronos post-mortem (published Sep 14)

This is a **publication**, not a new incident. The Tectonic exploit itself occurred on **2026-08-30 at 12:49:39 UTC** and the Cronos rollback ran on Aug 31 — it is not a Sep 14 event, and anyone reading headlines that day should not carry it forward as fresh. It is, however, the best technical write-up available in this window and it maps directly onto the SpiralCom incident above.

**What happened:** the attacker lifted the traded price of TONIC roughly **300×** over about seven minutes, then in a single transaction ran a recursive borrow-and-re-supply loop **98 iterations deep**, borrowing **$120.4M across nine Tectonic markets** against the inflated collateral. Cronos validators halted at block 90,907,150 and restarted at block 90,896,189, discarding **10,961 blocks (~114 minutes)** and restoring **$111.2M**. **$9.19M had already bridged out to Ethereum before the halt and remains unrecovered.**

**The stack of design failures, which is the part worth reading:**
- TONIC carried a **20% collateral factor** against only ~$1.34M of liquidity
- no cap tying TONIC collateral capacity to market depth
- no restriction on borrowing and re-supplying the *same* asset
- pricing off a thin on-chain pool and an internal feed rather than a purpose-built external oracle
- no circuit breaker on rapid collateral-price movement

**Bug class:** price-manipulation, collateral-parameter misconfiguration, recursive-collateral loop

**Sources:**

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. `tx.origin`-keyed same-block guards are not guards — sweep for them today**

SpiralCom is the cheap, clean demonstration of a pattern worth a systematic sweep. Any "you cannot swap and borrow in the same block" protection keyed on `tx.origin`, `msg.sender`, or a per-address flag is defeated by splitting the operation across N fresh EOAs. Grep your targets for:
- mappings of the form `lastActionBlock[tx.origin]` / `lastSwapBlock[msg.sender]` used to gate a *pricing-sensitive* function
- any `require(block.number > lastX[...])` where the key is attacker-choosable
- Uniswap V4 hooks that enforce per-caller rate limits rather than per-pool state limits

The correct guard is on **pool state** (did the pool's price move this block?), not on the caller's identity. Pair this with the SpiralCom root cause: `getSlot0()` used for collateral valuation with no TWAP and no deviation bound. That combination — spot pricing plus an identity-keyed guard — is a complete exploit on its own, and V4 hooks are a young, under-audited surface with a lot of it.

**2. Thin-liquidity collateral + recursive supply: reproduce the Tectonic parameter audit on every lending market you cover**

The Tectonic write-up gives you a checklist you can run against any Compound/Aave fork in under an hour, and it is a *parameter* audit, not a code audit — most audits do not cover it:
- For each listed collateral: what is `collateralFactor` × circulating value versus the asset's actual on-chain liquidity? Tectonic listed a token at 20% CF against ~$1.34M of depth.
- Can the same asset be borrowed and re-supplied as collateral? If yes, the effective leverage is `1/(1-CF)` per loop with no natural bound — 98 iterations fit in one transaction.
- Is the price for that asset sourced from a pool the borrower can move? A 300× pump over seven minutes was enough.
- Is there any per-asset borrow cap or supply cap tying exposure to measured depth?

Governance-token collateral in a protocol's own lending market is the archetype. It is usually listed for ecosystem reasons and parameterised optimistically.

**3. Audit the off-chain relayer's data source, not just its contract (Long bridge)**

The Long bridge lost money with **zero** contract bugs and **zero** key compromise. For every bridge, keeper, or cross-chain relayer in scope, treat the RPC boundary as an attack surface:
- Where does the keeper read source-chain events from? A single third-party public RPC is a single point of forgery.
- Is there a fallback chain of endpoints, and what happens when the primary is saturated or rate-limited? Long's failure path was exactly this — backup overwhelmed, primary returned forged logs.
- Does the keeper verify a **proof** (light client, Merkle inclusion against a known header) or does it trust `eth_getLogs` output on its face?
- Are the keeper's safety checks limited to replay/double-payment? Those are orthogonal to provenance and will happily approve a first-time payout against a fabricated event.

Ask for the keeper's config and endpoint list during scoping. This class does not show up in a Solidity-only review, and bounty programmes frequently leave off-chain infrastructure ambiguously in scope — clarify it before you spend time.

**4. Signed-payload malleability on non-EVM legs (Chainflip)**

Chainflip's loss came from appending a memo to an **already-signed** transaction and having the protocol's monitor read the new memo as a distinct event. For any protocol with a TRON, Solana, Cosmos, or Bitcoin leg, ask: what exactly does the validator signature cover, and what can be appended, reordered, or mutated afterwards without invalidating it? Then ask the second question, which is where the money is: does the **monitoring/indexing** logic derive swap identity from a field the signature does not cover? A refund path that triggers on a "failed swap" heuristic derived from mutable metadata is a double-payout waiting to happen.
