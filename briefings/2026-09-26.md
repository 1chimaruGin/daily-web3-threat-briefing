# Web3 Threat Briefing — 2026-09-26

> **Scope:** Multi-chain (EVM/Solidity · Solana/Rust · Cosmos/Move · cross-chain bridges). Priority: DeFi, wallets, key infrastructure.
> **Window:** Last ~24–48h (September 24–26 UTC). **Headline: Bitget's root cause landed — not a stolen private key, but a compromised backend system that spoofed transaction data past its own authorization process — while the loss estimate climbed to ~$387.5M and Elliptic independently rated North Korean involvement "highly likely." Two fresh bridge incidents (Payy on Ethereum, Meter.io on BNB Chain) both point to verification-logic bugs rather than key theft, a different failure mode than most of the week's headlines.**

## 1. 🔴 Active exploits (last 24h)

### Bitget — Backend Authorization Spoofing, ~$387.5M (revised, company-reported)

**Chain:** Multi-chain exchange infrastructure — assets drained across Ethereum, BNB Chain, Avalanche, TRON and Zcash (ETH, BNB, AVAX, USDT, USDT0, USDC, XAUT, XRP, ZEC, TRX reported)
**Status:** CONFIRMED breach, root cause now disclosed (partially), total revised upward. Detected **Sept 24, 18:31 UTC**. On **Sept 25**, Bitget raised its loss estimate from $351.6M to **$387.5M**, attributing the increase to "a more complete accounting of transfers," including previously uncounted Zcash and TRON legs. Bitget says the incident is contained (no further unauthorized transfers) and launched a two-tier Recovery Bounty Program (5% for freezing attacker funds, 5% for recovery directly resulting from voluntary assistance; law-enforcement-driven recoveries don't qualify). Withdrawals remain suspended; Bitget said it would announce a withdrawal-resumption plan by **Sept 26, ~04:00 UTC** — not yet independently confirmed as published at the time of writing.
**Impact:** ~$387.5M by Bitget's own count. The single largest component is now reported as **~103M XRP (~$157.5M)**, a figure on-chain tracker Lookonchain has separately corroborated — closing part of the gap flagged in yesterday's briefing between the company total and independent tracing. The remainder of the total (non-XRP legs) has not been independently re-verified against the revised figure. Cold wallets remain reported unaffected.
**What happened:** Bitget's account, per CEO Gracy Chen, is that attackers compromised a backend system inside its wallet infrastructure, used it to fabricate ("spoof") transaction data, and pushed that fabricated data through Bitget's own withdrawal-authorization pipeline — functionally similar to forging an internal transfer request that then sails through legitimate approval steps. Chen was explicit: "They did not forge user withdrawal requests, nor did they obtain our private keys."
**Root cause:** Access-control failure in backend transaction-authorization logic, not private-key compromise. Bitget has not disclosed the specific flaw that let spoofed data pass validation, nor how the attacker obtained backend access in the first place. Mandiant and SlowMist are assisting the forensic investigation.
**Attribution:** North Korea suspected by Bitget's CEO based on VPN/IP indicators and pattern-matching to prior incidents (**alleged**, CEO-stated). Independently, blockchain analytics firm **Elliptic assessed the attribution as "highly likely"**, citing on-chain links between the stolen XRP/ETH and addresses tied to a prior DPRK-attributed theft, and Bitget proceeds touching addresses previously used to launder the 2025 $1.4B Bybit heist. Elliptic says this pushes North Korea's confirmed/suspected 2026 crypto theft total past $1B — the largest single suspected DPRK theft of the year. This is a materially stronger corroboration than yesterday's CEO-only claim, but is still an assessment ("highly likely"), not a forensic certainty.
**Bug class:** access-control (backend transaction-authorization/validation bypass via spoofed transaction data).

### Payy Network — Ethereum Rollup Bridge Drain, ~$1.92M USDC

**Chain:** Ethereum (Payy Network / Payy Wallet non-custodial rollup)
**Status:** CONFIRMED exploit, root cause narrowed but not fully published. Exploited **Sept 24, starting 04:21 UTC**, with a second payout around 09:30 UTC the same day. Payy froze deposits, withdrawals, transfers and card payments network-wide. On **Sept 25**, Payy said its first-stage investigation was complete and it is now validating findings with a third-party audit firm, with a full report expected "in the next few days." No restoration timeline or user-reimbursement statement has been given.
**Impact:** ~$1.92M in USDC (1,828,589 USDC + 90,202 USDC across two transactions) left Payy's bridge contract to a single attacker-controlled address, funded in part via the Railgun privacy protocol. Payy describes the funds as users' non-custodial deposits — i.e., customer funds, not just protocol treasury.
**What happened:** Both payouts were executed through `verifyRollup`, the function Payy uses to post batches of network activity to its Ethereum bridge, and both were submitted by the same address that had legitimately posted 200+ consecutive batches since late August — meaning the attacker either forged a payload that this trusted poster address accepted as valid, or otherwise abused the batch-verification path itself.
**Root cause:** Payy has explicitly **ruled out** a compromised signing key, social engineering, and exploitation of off-chain infrastructure — narrowing the cause toward the on-chain rollup-proof/batch-verification logic itself. The precise flaw has not been published pending third-party audit confirmation.
**Bug class:** bridge-verification-logic (rollup batch-posting/proof-verification path allowed an unauthorized payout despite ruling out key theft).

### Meter.io — Meter Passport Bridge Exploit, ~$2.3M Unbacked wMTRG (BNB Chain)

**Chain:** BNB Chain (Meter Passport bridge; Meter mainnet)
**Status:** Reported ONGOING at last check. Attack began **Sept 23, ~21:14 UTC** (some outlets report ~18:14 UTC; exact start time not fully reconciled across sources) and was flagged by Blockaid as an active exploit on Sept 24. Meter's mainnet and the Passport bridge were paused in response; no confirmed-fixed statement or full post-mortem was found as of this writing, and Meter had not detailed how the attacker gained minting capability.
**Impact:** ~$2.3M in unbacked wrapped MTRG (wMTRG) minted on BNB Chain across roughly two large mint transactions, with the attacker selling portions on PancakeSwap. MTR and MTRG token prices both crashed (MTRG down as much as ~72-75% within 24h).
**What happened:** The attacker minted wMTRG on BNB Chain without depositing the corresponding native MTRG collateral through Meter Passport, then dumped the unbacked tokens on-chain for real assets — a classic bridge-mint/collateral-mismatch pattern.
**Root cause:** Meter has characterized it as abuse of a **block-validation flaw** on the Meter mainnet (some reporting references missing boundary checks during deserialization of externally supplied data, echoing the mechanism of Meter Passport's 2022 exploit, though Meter has not confirmed whether this is the same bug class recurring). Full technical root cause not yet published.
**Bug class:** block-validation / bridge-mint-authorization (cross-chain minting not properly gated on verified collateral deposit).

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Backend transaction-authorization pipelines, not just key custody**

Bitget's disclosed root cause is a genuinely different failure mode than most exchange breaches: no key was stolen, but a backend system that *prepares* transaction data for the signing/authorization pipeline was compromised, and the fabricated data was accepted as legitimate downstream. If scope includes exchange or custodial infrastructure, push past "who holds the signing key" and ask: what internal systems generate or validate the *data* that authorization logic trusts, and what stops a compromised internal service from feeding it forged-but-well-formed withdrawal instructions?

**2. Bridge/rollup "trusted poster" functions — verification logic, not just the poster's key**

Payy's incident is the clean counter-example to the assume-it's-always-key-theft pattern: the batch-posting address itself was not compromised (per Payy's own denial), yet `verifyRollup` still paid out to an attacker. Any bridge or rollup contract with a single trusted "poster" or "relayer" role is worth auditing for whether the verification function actually validates the *content* of what's posted, or merely checks that the caller is the expected address — the latter is a spoofing surface even without stealing that address's key.

**3. Cross-chain mint authorization tied to block/light-client validation**

Meter.io's Passport bridge let wMTRG mint on BNB Chain without corresponding collateral, attributed to a block-validation flaw — the same general bug class (light-client/header-validation bugs enabling unbacked minting) that has repeatedly hit bridges historically. Any bridge minting wrapped assets based on source-chain block or transaction proofs is worth checking for boundary/deserialization handling on externally supplied proof data.

**4. Don't take exchange-reported loss figures at face value until independently traced — but check whether the gap has closed**

Bitget's figure moved from $351.6M to $387.5M in a day, and the XRP-specific leg (~$157.5M) is now separately corroborated by Lookonchain — track whether a company's revision converges with independent on-chain tracing (a good sign) or diverges further (a red flag).

**5. Chain-level governance authority overriding app-level admin/multisig controls (Cosmos/CosmWasm) — still unresolved**

Neutron's `MsgUpdateAdmin` privilege-escalation path (Day 4, ~$9.4M, no post-mortem yet) remains the clearest live lesson: calculate the ratio between the cost of winning a governance vote at spot price right before tally and the value of assets that vote's outcome can control, for any Cosmos-SDK/CosmWasm protocol in scope. Check for timelocks between proposal tally and execution.

**6. Client-side signing implementations in hardware/software wallets — blast radius still growing**

DCENT's standing XRP-leg tracing (Day 12) remains the example: treat any "preliminary" loss figure attached to a client-side key-compromise incident as a floor, not a ceiling, and prioritize signing/key-derivation code review in any hardware or mobile wallet audit.
