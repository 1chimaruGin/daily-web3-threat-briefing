## 1. 🔴 Active exploits (last 24h)

### GaslessReservoirEnabler — Allowance-Binding Authorization Flaw, ~$23K

**Chain:** Polygon
**Status:** CONFIRMED, first disclosed Sept 21. Not previously captured in this briefing series — reported now as newly identified.
**Impact:** ~$23,000 across roughly 997 token-holder addresses.
**What happened:** The contract's `erc20WithTransfersAndExecute` → `_executeInternal` path checked that the calling module address was whitelisted, but did not bind the resulting `transferFrom` call to the asset owner who had granted the allowance. Any caller routed through a whitelisted module could spend a victim's pre-existing ERC-20 allowance (WETH, ZED) against the victim's wallet rather than their own. Proceeds were consolidated and moved through a bridge on Polygon.
**Root cause:** authorization/binding gap — module-address whitelisting was mistaken for authorization of the underlying token movement, a classic confused-deputy pattern in gasless/meta-transaction forwarders.
**Bug class:** access-control (allowance not bound to authorized owner).

### DoinGud — Diamond Bidding Contract Replay, ~$35.5K

**Chain:** Polygon
**Status:** CONFIRMED, first disclosed Sept 21. Not previously captured in this briefing series — reported now as newly identified.
**Impact:** ~$35,486 in USDC.e.
**What happened:** DoinGud is a Polygon NFT platform that appears dormant since roughly 2024 but still held live contract balances. Its Diamond-pattern bidding contract's `acceptBid` function paid out the bid amount but never cleared the underlying bid record, so the same bid could be accepted more than once. The attacker flash-loaned USDC equal to the contract's balance, placed a bid on themselves, then called `acceptBid` twice against the same unlcleared record to drain the contract.
**Root cause:** missing state invalidation after payout — a pay-then-forget-to-clear bug that allows replay of an already-settled action, amplified here with a flash loan to size the extraction to the full contract balance in one transaction.
**Bug class:** logic-error (state not invalidated after payout, enabling replay).

**Sources:**

**Status:** STILL HALTED as of Sept 23 reporting. First disclosed **Sept 19**; cause confirmed **Sept 20**. Loss amount still **not disclosed**.

**Root cause:** still not publicly detailed beyond "VM-level atomicity" — treat as an unpatched client/VM implementation bug until MultiversX publishes its promised technical report.

## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

### Pragma flags 6 Starknet price feeds as "critical risk" post-Nostra

**Published Sept 22.** Starknet oracle provider Pragma reviewed its 22 mainnet price feeds after the Nostra NSTR exploit (above) and flagged **6 as critical risk: BROTHER, DAI, DOG, EKUBO, LORDS and NSTR**. The core finding: a quoted oracle price does not prove the underlying collateral is actually liquid. Pragma measured that selling just $10,000 of the flagged tokens caused price impact of 15% (NSTR), 17% (EKUBO), 22% (LORDS) and 20% (BROTHER) — meaning lending markets pricing these tokens at their oracle quote are overstating realizable collateral value. Pragma's remediation guidance: enforce a **minimum of three independent sources** per price feed (the manipulated NSTR feed had relied on two), add freshness checks and risk-appropriate thresholds, and have lending protocols separately evaluate exit liquidity before accepting an asset as collateral, independent of its listed price. This is a direct, actionable advisory for any Starknet lending-market audit: check both the oracle's source count and whether the protocol's collateral-listing process accounts for market depth, not just price availability.

**Sources:**
- [CryptoSlate — Pragma flags 6 price feeds as critical risk following $3.5M Starknet lending exploit](https://cryptoslate.com/pragma-flags-6-price-feeds-as-critical-risk-following-3-5m-starknet-lending-exploit/)

### Darksword iOS exploit chain — unverified claim it now targets iOS 26.5 wallet apps

**Reported Sept 21–22, disputed detail below.** SlowMist's CISO (23pds) claimed that Darksword — a full-chain iOS exploit combining six vulnerabilities, originally disclosed by Google's Threat Intelligence Group in **March 2026** and historically limited to iOS 18.4–18.7 — has been adapted by attackers to target **iOS 26.5**, with reported campaigns going after Keychain data, file access and keyboard input while wallet apps including **imToken, TokenPocket and TronLink** were in the foreground. SlowMist explicitly flags this iOS 26.5 adaptation as **not yet independently verified**. Darksword itself does not break wallet cryptography — it targets the window where a wallet app holds decrypted private key material in device memory, letting an attacker who has compromised the device read or intercept it.

**Caveat on the widely-repeated $1.8M loss figure:** multiple outlets covering this Darksword story cite "three investors" losing "$1.8 million" via a fake wallet app downloaded from the Apple App Store. Independent research shows this exact fact pattern (three plaintiffs, $1.8M combined, fake **Sparrow Wallet** app) matches a **separate, unrelated lawsuit against Apple filed around July 27, 2026** over App Store screening failures — not a confirmed Darksword-attributed loss. Treat the $1.8M figure as misattributed until a source directly ties it to the Darksword exploit chain rather than the Sparrow Wallet impersonation case.

**Bug class:** client-implementation (private key material held in accessible device memory; OS-level exploit chain, not a wallet-app logic bug per se)

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Allowance-binding gaps in gasless/meta-transaction forwarders**

GaslessReservoirEnabler's flaw is a clean confused-deputy: the contract checked that the *caller* (a module) was whitelisted, but never bound the resulting `transferFrom` to the *owner* who granted the allowance. Any contract that relays token movement on behalf of users via pre-approved allowances — meta-tx relayers, gasless routers, permit-based forwarders — needs the same check: does authorization of the calling module also imply authorization of *whose* allowance gets spent? If those are decoupled, any whitelisted module becomes a way to drain arbitrary approvers.

**2. Payout functions that don't invalidate the state that gated them**

DoinGud's `acceptBid` paid out but never cleared the bid record, so it could be replayed. This is a general pattern worth sweeping for in any "accept/claim/redeem/execute" function: after value moves out, does the contract delete, zero-out, or otherwise invalidate the record that authorized the payout? If not, and especially if a flash loan can size the attacker's position to the contract's full balance, this is a live finding — not just theoretical, given it just paid out on a dormant NFT platform's contract that nobody had touched in two years.

**3. Oracle price availability vs. realizable liquidity**

Pragma's post-Nostra review is a direct, quantified template for oracle audits: a price feed can return a clean, timely quote from a legitimate DEX pool and still be attackable if that pool is thin enough to move 15–22% on a $10K sell. Check any lending market's collateral list against (a) how many independent sources back each feed, and (b) whether the protocol separately gates collateral eligibility on market depth, not just price availability. Nostra's NSTR feed had two sources; Pragma's floor recommendation is three.

**4. Client-side key material residency in mobile wallets**

Darksword doesn't attack wallet logic — it attacks the fact that decrypted private keys sit in accessible device memory while a wallet app is in the foreground. If scope includes a mobile wallet's signing flow, check how long decrypted key material persists in memory, whether it's cleared immediately after signing, and whether the app can detect execution in a compromised OS environment (jailbreak/exploit indicators) rather than assuming a benign host OS.

**5. Shared-key blast-radius mapping — still the standing lesson from Fetch.ai/SingularityNET**

Unchanged guidance: before scoping a bridge/converter audit down to a single contract, enumerate every other contract, token and chain that trusts the same authorizer key or multisig. This incident widened three separate times as tracing firms found more tokens sharing the compromised key; assume any "one converter, one key" scoping is incomplete until independently verified.

**6. VM/client-layer atomicity — still open, still paying out in production**

MultiversX's mainnet has now been halted for five days on an unpublished VM-level atomicity bug, with restart timing still undetermined as of Sept 23. If scope includes execution-client or VM code, non-atomic state transitions remain a live, unresolved bug class — not a hypothetical one.
