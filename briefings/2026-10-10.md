### Ledger-Linked Hardware Wallets — Suspected Reseller Supply-Chain Compromise, >$86M (disputed, up to ~$93.4M) (Day 1)

**Chain:** Bitcoin, Ethereum, TRON (multi-chain victim wallets).
**Status:** Active investigation, first disclosed Oct 9, 2026. On-chain analyst Specter began tracking a wave of drains from Ledger hardware-wallet users, concentrated in Southeast Asia, with victims reporting empty wallets despite never signing a transaction and never exposing their seed phrase to their knowledge. Reported totals vary by outlet and by the hour: the commonly cited figure is "over $86M" (roughly $42M+ in ETH, ~$17.5M in BTC, ~$16.5M in USDT), with some reports pushing the estimate toward $93.4M as more victims surfaced. Treat the exact figure as unsettled and still climbing as of this writing.
**What happened / root cause:** Ledger identified the common thread as a Southeast Asian reseller, CryptoBilis, and has halted sales and shipments through that channel, advising anyone who bought a device from CryptoBilis in the last 90 days not to set it up, and anyone who already did to migrate funds to a new device with a fresh seed phrase. Ledger's own statement: "Ledger is investigating reports of loss of funds from users in South East Asia who purchased products from a reseller named CryptoBillis." The attack mechanism itself is **not confirmed** — candidate explanations circulating include tampered/counterfeit devices swapped in before reaching the customer, a malicious firmware implant, or pre-generated/exposed seed phrases baked in before sale. Ledger has explicitly not confirmed a vulnerability in its own hardware or firmware, and multiple outlets note there is no evidence to date of a flaw in genuine, untampered Ledger devices — this looks, so far, like a single-reseller supply-chain compromise rather than a platform-wide hardware bug. Binance founder Changpeng Zhao publicly characterized it as a supply-chain attack at one vendor; Mark Karpelès described the working theory as a hardware implant that passes the device's genuineness check.
**Bug class:** key-management / supply-chain (hardware-wallet reseller channel compromise; exact mechanism — firmware, physical tamper, or pre-exposed seed — unconfirmed).

### 79thVault — 79AU Pool Operator-Key Exploit on BNB Chain, ~$12.5M (~$11M Voluntarily Returned) (Day 4)

**Chain:** BNB Chain. 79thVault's 79AU token and its PancakeSwap liquidity pool.
**Status:** Missed from the Oct 7 window by the Oct 9 edition; added now with correct dating. Exploit executed Oct 7, 2026, ~07:25–08:19 UTC. On Oct 9, the attacker voluntarily returned 15,000 BNB (~$11M) to a project-designated wallet; roughly 89% of the original haul was recovered this way, though an earlier report also noted ~14,394.92 BNB sitting largely unmoved in a consolidation address before the return — the two figures likely describe the same returned balance from different snapshots, and the exact final unrecovered amount is not cleanly stated in available reporting.
**What happened / root cause:** A wallet holding `OPERATOR_ROLE` on the 79AU token contract called an unrestricted function that could move tokens out of the PancakeSwap liquidity pool without paying for them, then called `sync()` to force the pool's reserves to match the new (post-theft) balances. The attacker moved 2.01M 79AU tokens out in seven transactions, then sold them back into the same pool across roughly 95 swaps, draining the pool's USDT side from ~$15.2M to ~$3.9M and cashing out 16,249 BNB (~$12.5M). Security researchers (GoPlus) describe this as either a suspected operator-private-key compromise or possible insider action; 79thVault's own public statement was a generic "system upgrade" notice with no technical detail and no confirmation either way.
**Bug class:** access-control (privileged operator-role function with no balance/payment check, paired with a reserve-sync call that let the attacker legitimize the post-theft pool state).

### BitBay (Polygon) — Vault Zero-Liquidity Withdrawal Bypass, ~$14K (Day 1)

**Chain:** Polygon. BitBay's DAI/USDC vault.
**Status:** Closed, single-day. Flagged by SlowMist on Oct 9, 2026.
**What happened / root cause:** The vault's `_withdraw()` function transferred the contract's *entire* token balance whenever pool `liquidity == 0`, instead of honoring the caller's proportional share. The attacker called `reposition()` to drive liquidity to zero, then redeemed a small share and received the vault's full remaining balance — 14,838.47 DAI — rather than their actual entitlement.
**Bug class:** logic-error / invariant-validation (withdrawal function with an unguarded zero-liquidity edge case that skips share-ratio accounting entirely).

### EtherVista — Swap Pool Integer-Overflow, ~$18.6K (+ Follow-On MEV-Bot Drains) (Day 1)

**Chain:** Ethereum. EtherVistaPair pool contract.
**Status:** Closed, single-day. Flagged by SlowMist on Oct 9, 2026, within roughly two hours of the BitBay alert above.
**What happened / root cause:** `EtherVistaPair.swap()`'s invariant (K-value) check multiplies the pool's two `uint112` reserves; the attacker's trade sizing caused that multiplication to overflow and wrap around, letting the check pass even though the real product of reserves had dropped well below the required invariant. After registering a self-controlled contract as an authorized router, the attacker drained WETH and VISTA from the pool (~10 ETH initially); MEV bots then discovered and repeated the identical overflow in the following block, draining a further ~20 ETH before the hole could be addressed. Combined BitBay + EtherVista losses this window: ~$32,600.
**Bug class:** logic-error / arithmetic (fixed-width integer overflow defeating a constant-product invariant check).

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Reseller/supply-chain integrity for hardware wallets, now with a live, unresolved case (Ledger/CryptoBilis, new this window)**

A single reseller's device channel is the suspected point of compromise for a $86M+ drain, with Ledger explicitly not able to rule in or out firmware tampering, physical implants, or pre-exposed seeds. If you audit wallet or custody infrastructure, this is a concrete, current example of why "the device passed the genuineness check" is not sufficient assurance — ask what tamper-evidence exists between manufacture and the box reaching the end user, and whether a reseller relationship could become an attack surface on its own.

**2. Privileged "operator" or "admin" roles that can move pooled funds without a payment check (79thVault, new this window)**

79thVault's `OPERATOR_ROLE` function could pull tokens out of a liquidity pool and then call `sync()` to make the post-theft state look legitimate. For any protocol with an operator/admin/keeper role touching a pool or vault balance: check whether that role's functions enforce any form of payment, collateral, or balance-conservation check, or whether they simply trust the caller because of the role tag — and whether a `sync()`-style reserve-refresh function can be used afterward to erase the evidence of imbalance.

**3. Fixed-width integer arithmetic inside invariant checks (EtherVista, new this window)**

A classic but still-live bug class: a `uint112`-based K-value (constant-product) check that overflows under attacker-chosen trade sizes, letting the invariant pass when it shouldn't. Worth a dedicated sweep on any AMM-style pool using fixed-width reserve types — check every multiplication in the invariant check for overflow potential, not just the obvious reserve updates. Note also that MEV bots found and repeated this exact bug within one block of the first exploit — assume any live, unpatched bug of this kind will be found and drained again, fast.

**4. Zero-balance / zero-liquidity edge cases in withdrawal logic (BitBay, new this window)**

A withdrawal function that pays out the vault's *entire* balance instead of a proportional share whenever liquidity hits exactly zero. For any vault or pool: explicitly test the liquidity == 0 edge case on every withdrawal/redeem path, not just the common case — and check whether an attacker can cheaply force that edge case themselves (here, via a single `reposition()` call).

**5. AI-generated research-summary conflation is a recurring, now twice-caught failure mode (Aave FlashLoopAdapter, standing)**

Two editions in a row, independent searches for "did the Aave FlashLoopAdapter attacker return funds" surfaced an AI-written summary that actually describes NEAR Intents' unrelated $3.8M return (quoting GM Alex Shevchenko). If you're using AI search/summarization tools to track bounty or fund-return outcomes for your own targets, verify the entity name in the primary quote matches the incident you're researching — don't trust a confident-sounding "funds returned in full" without checking which fund return it's actually citing.

**6. Governance-controlled recovery still has an open middle step (Neutron, standing)**

Prop 1058 is still in voting with no published result — don't assume a long voting period means stalled or dead; check the proposal page directly if this matters to your work.

**7. Post-mortem latency as its own signal (standing)**

Meter.io, DCENT, Fetch.ai, MultiversX, Blink, MALT, GoldPesa, 79thVault and now the Ledger/CryptoBilis incident all currently lack (or substantially lack) a primary technical post-mortem, several well overdue. Reason from on-chain evidence and third-party analysis (SlowMist, GoPlus, Specter, DeFiHackLabs) rather than waiting, but keep re-checking — fuller technical pictures have repeatedly emerged well after the initial, thinner reporting this cycle.
