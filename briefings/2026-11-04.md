### Binance — Full Root-Cause Chain Disclosed: Fake Interview, Tampered Homebrew, AI-Tool Pivot, 50-Signature EIP-7702 Drain (Day 6)

**Chain:** Ethereum (site of the EIP-7702 delegation attack and the bulk of the technical mechanism described below); multi-chain impact otherwise unchanged (Bitcoin, Tron, XRP Ledger, Solana, Optimism, plus Arbitrum/Base/Avalanche/Polygon per prior TRM Labs trace).
**Status:** Recovery essentially complete per Binance's own figures; root-cause disclosure now public for the first time via a media briefing; attribution actively disputed by North Korea's own government.
**Impact:** Total theft figure remains cited inconsistently across sources in the $6.3-6.43B range, not reconciled to one number by this series. Binance's CEO/co-CEO Richard Teng told the briefing "about $2.85 billion has been frozen" and "about 98% of the affected tokens have been replenished with company funds" — an increase from the 95%-asset-coverage figure reported as of Nov 1. CoinDesk separately reports Binance's net outflows reached approximately $5.3 billion following the hack, though hourly deposits overtook withdrawals by 03:00 UTC on Nov 3, read as a sentiment-stabilization signal rather than a loss figure.
**What happened (full chain, disclosed for the first time this window):** At a media briefing, Binance's chief security officer Jimmy Su and Richard Teng described, in Su's words, "a campaign, built over many months." The timeline as disclosed:
- **September 2026:** A software engineer was contacted through a legitimate job platform by someone posing as a project director. A fake HR representative conducted a video interview and shared a social-media account with personal photos to build trust; a second interview, with a purported CEO, ended with the engineer told to download a "coding assignment" onto his work computer. That assignment "contained zero-day malware that evaded endpoint security," per Su, and is separately described as having "swapped a developer tool for a rogue copy" to evade EDR.
- **June 2026 (a separate, earlier thread):** Another engineer had installed a tampered Homebrew package while searching for an AI coding tool. Binance says it caught and replaced that device, but the engineer and his colleagues on the wallet team remained exposed afterward.
- **Oct 20, 2026:** Per Su, "attackers used the machine compromised by the fake interview and an internal AI tool to reach two wallet engineers' computers." Neither the specific internal AI tool nor the exact abuse mechanism is named in available reporting; one of the two reached engineers held a container-deployment token/credential.
- **Oct 29, 2026:** Attackers used that stolen credential "to deploy a container and run a malicious script." Funds began leaving at 20:05 UTC.
**Root cause — the EIP-7702 mechanism, now explained in detail:** Su stated: "The attacker used an Ethereum feature called EIP-7702 to gain execution rights over a hot wallet... Because Binance's hot wallets use multiparty computation, no single key could revoke it, and containment took until 00:10 UTC on Oct. 30" — a roughly four-hour-five-minute window. Per on-chain reconstruction attributed to TRM Labs and Elliptic (via secondary technical reporting, not Binance's own primary disclosure): a single Ethereum transaction carried **50 separate EIP-7702 authorization signatures**, all signed by the hot wallet Elliptic labels "**Binance 14**," each pointing at the same attacker-controlled sweeper contract. Because a signed EIP-7702 authorization is only valid if its embedded nonce matches the account's live nonce counter at execution time, and Binance 14 is a busy wallet whose nonce advances continuously, the attacker could not know in advance which nonce would be live — the working theory (explicitly flagged by the reporting outlet as analysis, not a confirmed fact) is that the 50 signatures covered a range of plausible nonces, so that "whichever one matched the live counter took effect." Once any one of those delegations was accepted, the wallet's EOA behaved, in effect, as if it had signed "a permanent power of attorney": every subsequent call to the wallet executed the attacker's sweeper-contract logic with no further signature required, and this delegated execution right "operated independently of Binance's normal withdrawal infrastructure" — meaning Binance suspending withdrawals could not stop every transfer, which is the proximate reason containment took roughly four hours rather than being immediate. Within that window, five hot wallets sent 233.46 million XRP to an attacker address in four minutes, followed by Solana, Optimism and Bitcoin wallet drains. Teng was explicit that producing 50 delegation signatures from the signing pipeline without the pattern being flagged points to an access-control failure in whatever service was trusted to request and issue those signatures, not a cryptographic break of EIP-7702 itself or (per Binance) classic raw private-key theft: "no private keys appear to have leaked," and "the attacker did not simply use one engineer's permissions, and this was not a single 'one-click' action."
**Remediation:** Binance says it has rebuilt the wallet system and the implicated internal AI tool, replaced affected devices, revoked related credentials, and is bringing in independent outside security firms for review. Binance states it blocked 57 suspected North Korea-linked intrusion attempts in 2026 separate from this incident, and has committed to covering all user losses.
**Attribution — actively disputed by name:** Binance's briefing reiterated the FBI's North Korea/TraderTraitor attribution. North Korea's own government responded directly this window: Kim Yo Jong, sister of Kim Jong Un, called the FBI's claim a "grave political provocation" and said, per multiple outlets' translation, "The FBI is trying to brand the DPRK as a 'cyber criminal state'... obtained information that a hacking organization 'supported' by the DPRK was involved," accusing Washington of misleadingly attributing global cybercrime to North Korea by default. South Korean commentary described her direct, named intervention in a cybersecurity dispute as unprecedented — read as a measure of how reputationally damaging Pyongyang considers this specific allegation, not as evidence either way on the underlying attribution. TRM Labs and Chainalysis/Elliptic both continue to maintain their own independent North Korea attribution regardless of the FBI's or North Korea's statements.
**Bug class:** key-management / EIP-7702 account-abstraction delegation feature weaponized after a trusted signing/approval-pipeline credential was compromised via a multi-month social-engineering and supply-chain campaign — not a flaw in EIP-7702 itself, but a demonstration that account-delegation primitives create a persistence mechanism that bypasses withdrawal-suspension-based incident response once an attacker reaches signature-issuing infrastructure.

**Status:** Active investigation and fund-tracing continuing; deposits/withdrawals remain suspended; no root cause beyond a preliminary characterization has been published.
**Impact:** Four separate trackers now give four different, explicitly unreconciled figures: Mercado Bitcoin's own ~$38 million, Hypernative's >$40 million, Arkham analyst Emmett Gallic's ~$46 million (a gap of at least $8 million versus the exchange's own figure that the exchange has not explained), and Chainalysis's ~$49 million across the 17 chains. ## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

**CryptoPayment Gateway WordPress plugin — CVE-2026-81648, CVSS 10.0, no patch, renewed exploitation-attempt reports:** Versions 1.2.1-1.2.2 of the CryptoPayment Gateway plugin expose an unauthenticated AJAX router at `wp-content/plugins/cryptopayment-gateway/vendor/cryptd/ajax.php` that applies no authorization check at all (CWE-862/CWE-284). A verified WPScan proof-of-concept shows an unauthenticated caller can (1) read the plugin's stored settings, (2) overwrite those settings including wallet-address/key fields, (3) recover the stored wallet key in cleartext via the plugin's own exposed decryption function, and (4) delete arbitrary files via unsanitized path traversal in a `delete-file` function — including `wp-config.php`, which forces the site into WordPress's setup wizard and allows full site takeover by an attacker who points it at a database they control. The vulnerability was originally disclosed by researcher Pedro Pinho and published to WPScan around Sept 10-13, 2026; it carries a "no known fix" status as of this writing, and the plugin was separately removed from the WordPress Plugin Directory on Aug 28, 2026. The news hook for this window is renewed activity: The Hacker News and multiple secondary outlets reported Nov 3 that security firm Defused Cyber flagged active exploitation *attempts* against the still-unpatched flaw — this series could not independently fetch The Hacker News' primary article (repeated 403 errors) and relies on secondary corroboration (TokenPost, CryptoTimes) for the exploitation-attempt claim; no confirmed fund loss has been attributed to in-the-wild exploitation of this CVE as of writing. Mitigation in the absence of a patch: remove the plugin entirely, block the vulnerable endpoint and its companion `upload.php` at the web-server/WAF layer, and rotate any wallet credentials that may have been stored in it.

**Solana validator Linux kernel flaw — CVE-2026-98432, CVSS 9.8, Ubuntu unpatched, no confirmed exploitation:** Anza warned Solana mainnet-beta validators on Nov 1 to take immediate protective action against a critical Linux kernel vulnerability arising from incorrect memory handling while processing IPv4 TCP connections, which could let an attacker remotely take control of an affected server. The upstream Linux kernel already contains a fix; Red Hat and AWS (Amazon Linux 2/2023) have shipped patched kernels, but Ubuntu — the OS Anza requires for mainnet-beta validators with public IPv4 connectivity — remained unpatched as of this reporting. No evidence has been reported of any Solana validator actually being compromised through this vulnerability. Interim mitigation for Ubuntu operators: filter incoming traffic and disable two specific networking features pending a fixed kernel release.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Account-abstraction delegation (EIP-7702 and equivalents) as a persistence mechanism once a signing pipeline is compromised (Binance, this window's dominant technical lesson)**

The headline fact isn't that EIP-7702 has a bug — it doesn't, per every source consulted. The lesson is that a legitimate account-delegation primitive, once an attacker reaches a system trusted to request and obtain signatures, converts a transient credential compromise into **persistent, withdrawal-suspension-independent execution rights** over an EOA. For any wallet, exchange, or custodian audit touching EVM infrastructure post-Prague: explicitly test whether your incident-response playbook assumes "suspend withdrawals/rotate keys" is sufficient to stop an active drain — it is not, if the attacker has already installed an EIP-7702 delegation. Concretely hunt for: (a) signing services that will produce repeated, similar authorization-signing requests (e.g., the same delegation target, varying only the nonce) without rate-limiting or pattern detection; (b) absence of a delegation allowlist restricting which contracts an account's signing infrastructure will ever authorize; (c) monitoring gaps that wouldn't flag an EIP-7702 "set code" transaction as materially different from a normal transfer.

**2. Transaction-authorization-credential compromise as a distinct bug class from smart-contract exploits (Mercado Bitcoin, this window)**

Mercado Bitcoin's own reporting draws an explicit, useful line: this was not defeating cryptographic protections on-chain, but obtaining credentials that are themselves authorized to request signatures. If your scope includes infrastructure-adjacent bounty terms, the audit question is "who/what can request a signing operation, and what stops a compromised instance of that system from requesting an attacker-chosen one" — not just "is the contract logic correct."

**3. Reused vulnerability classes across adjacent products (BTCPay Server's LND-then-on-chain repeat)**

The same underlying flaw class reportedly hit BTCPay's Lightning Network Daemon integration in August before reappearing against on-chain hot wallets this window. For any self-hosted wallet/payment-infrastructure audit, explicitly check whether a vulnerability disclosed and "fixed" for one subsystem (e.g., Lightning) was actually eliminated at the shared root cause, or just patched in the one place it was first observed.

**4. Unauthenticated AJAX/API endpoints in crypto-handling CMS plugins (CryptoPayment Gateway CVE-2026-81648)**

A missing authorization check on a single AJAX router exposed cleartext wallet keys, config overwrite, and full site takeover via path traversal — a textbook CWE-862 finding, still unpatched two months after disclosure. Any bounty or audit scope touching WordPress/CMS-integrated payment plugins should specifically enumerate every AJAX/REST endpoint a plugin registers and verify each independently requires authentication — a single unguarded router undermines protections implemented correctly everywhere else (as this plugin's own `ajax1.php` sibling shows).

**5. Loss-figure fragmentation across trackers is itself a signal worth probing (Mercado Bitcoin's four unreconciled estimates)**

When an exchange's own internal figure diverges by $8M+ from an independent on-chain tracker with no public explanation, that gap often maps to a real scoping question — e.g., does the internal figure exclude certain wallet categories, time windows, or already-attempted-but-reverted transactions? For bug-bounty submissions involving fund-loss estimates, document your methodology explicitly enough that a third party could reproduce your number — the ambiguity here is exactly the kind of gap that complicates both incident response and insurance/compensation claims.
