### Gnosis Pay — Legacy Card-System Data Breach (No Confirmed Funds Loss)

**Chain:** Not a blockchain exploit — a data breach of the legacy B2C platform powering Gnosis Pay-branded debit cards, which link to self-custodied crypto wallets; included here given the direct relevance of a crypto-card issuer's customer and wallet-address data to the wallet/key-infrastructure priority of this briefing.
**Status:** Disclosed and contained; affected legacy system already scheduled for retirement Dec 20, 2026.
**Impact:** **$0 confirmed funds loss.** Gnosis Pay and The Block both confirm customer crypto and fiat funds were not affected. Gnosis Pay has not publicly itemized exactly which data categories (full card numbers, CVVs, names, home addresses, linked wallet addresses) were exposed or disclosed a total affected-user count; it says it emailed each affected customer individually with the specific information taken from their account, and separately warned customers to watch for misuse of "your address or details of your crypto holdings," suggesting those categories may be among what leaked.
**What happened:** Gnosis Pay's own monitoring systems detected unauthorized access on Nov 3, 2026, and the company says containment began within two hours. The breach hit "the system powering Gnosis Pay-branded cards (our B2C business line)" — a legacy platform that was already slated for retirement on Dec 20, 2026 — not the separate enterprise platform that powers Gnosis Pay's white-label card programs, which was unaffected. Public disclosure came Nov 6, 2026.
**Root cause:** Not disclosed. Neither Gnosis Pay's blog post nor independent reporting specifies the technical vulnerability or entry vector used against the legacy system.
**Remediation:** Affected security credentials replaced; the underlying vulnerability addressed; 3D Secure authentication made mandatory for all online card payments pending the legacy system's full retirement; increased monitoring of card activity and linked wallets; breach reported to partners, data-protection authorities and the card issuer; specialist monitoring arranged for misuse of exposed data; affected customers notified individually by email.
**Attribution:** None disclosed.
**Bug class:** data-exposure / legacy-system credential and card-data breach — notable for occurring on a system the company had already begun winding down, a reminder that deprecation timelines leave a live attack surface until the actual shutdown date.

### Binance — SEAL Estimates 8-Week Attacker Dwell Time (Disputed); Elliptic Traces $124M Laundered Into Bitcoin (Day 9)

**Chain:** Ethereum/multi-chain; new laundering legs via Bitcoin and THORChain.
**Status:** Recovery essentially complete per Binance's own figures; investigative and laundering-trace developments continue.
**Impact:** Nonprofit security collective SEAL (Security Alliance) published a report, covered Nov 6, estimating that North Korea-linked TraderTraitor held roughly **eight weeks** of access to Binance's systems — specifically a September-to-October window — before the Oct 29 exploit, based on a Binance engineer's device being compromised via a fake-recruitment scheme in September. Binance's own account partially disputes this: it says a broader campaign began in **June** (a tampered Homebrew install during installation of an AI coding tool, detected and remediated at the time but which let attackers gather intelligence on wallet-infrastructure staff), with a second device compromise in September, but that attackers did **not** gain the access needed to reach wallet operations until **Oct 20** — using the September foothold plus a compromised internal AI tool to compromise two wallet-infrastructure engineers' devices and locate a deployment-permission token used in the Oct 29 exploit. SEAL's report also estimated dwell times for other recent incidents: ~6 weeks of prior access before the $388M Bitget hack, ~6 weeks of LayerZero access before the exploit that led to Kelp DAO's $293M loss, and ~3 weeks before the $24M AFX exploit. Separately, blockchain-analytics firm Elliptic reported (Nov 6) that attackers have swapped roughly **$124 million** of stolen altcoins into Bitcoin — about $50M in XRP via THORChain (with ~$226M in XRP still unconverted as of Nov 6) and nearly all stolen Solana already mixed/swapped/bridged into BTC — on top of the $1.48B in Bitcoin taken directly in the original heist. The operation has used over 20,000 wallets, 16 blockchains and roughly 65,000 transactions to obscure fund flow; Elliptic says only a "negligible" amount has likely been cashed out so far and expects the remaining stolen ether to be swapped in small batches before eventual cash-out via OTC brokers and Chinese money-laundering networks, and explicitly denies AI-agent involvement in the laundering decisions. Of the $6.43B total breach figure, $2.85B is frozen and $3.11B remains in attacker-linked addresses — a roughly $470M gap that Elliptic's reporting does not reconcile (possibly price moves, already-cashed-out funds, or both). Binance stated via X (Nov 6) it is working to reopen withdrawals for HYPE, INJ, APT, PLASMA, NEAR and LTC "by the end of next week," in addition to all tokens on already-restored networks (ETH, BSC, Arbitrum, Optimism).
**Root cause:** Unchanged (EIP-7702 delegation abuse following a compromised signing-pipeline credential). This window's developments are investigative/forensic — a disputed dwell-time estimate and a laundering-conversion trace — not new technical disclosures.
**Remediation:** Unchanged from prior windows.
**Attribution:** Unchanged — FBI/TRM Labs/Chainalysis maintain North Korea/TraderTraitor attribution; North Korea continues to deny involvement.
**Bug class:** Unchanged (key-management / EIP-7702 delegation persistence mechanism); this window adds a broader pattern — SEAL's cross-incident dwell-time estimates suggest multi-week undetected attacker access is common across several of 2026's largest crypto hacks, not unique to Binance.

## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

**Reth execution-client state-root bug — patched, no funds lost:** The Tempo development team discovered, via automated fuzz testing, that Reth's legacy state-root calculation code could produce an incorrect state root for certain smart-contract storage layouts when triggered by a specially crafted transaction; this legacy code path was used in block-building on lower-end hardware and on OP Stack chains. The fix (Reth PR #28264) merged Nov 3, 2026 at 21:00 UTC; patches shipped as Reth v2.10.0 (high priority), op-reth v2.8.1, Base node-reth v1.5.3, and an unversioned Tempo patch, with public disclosure (via Paradigm CTO Georgios Konstantopoulos) and BNB Chain/Base/Mantle coordination following Nov 4-5. LayerZero paused some cross-chain routes and Gauntlet restricted V1 vault deposits as precautions. No confirmed exploitation or fund loss — this was a responsibly-disclosed-and-patched bug, not an attack. Relevance for hunters: legacy/fallback code paths in execution clients (used only under specific hardware or chain conditions) are exactly the kind of under-tested surface fuzzing is built to find — worth checking whether any client or L2 your target depends on still runs the unpatched legacy calculator.

**DarkSword iOS exploit chain — already patched, re-flagged as a live risk to unpatched devices:** CertiK warned on Nov 6, 2026 that DarkSword, a zero-click exploit chain triggered simply by opening a malicious website in Safari (no wallet connection or transaction signature required), continues to drain cryptocurrency from iPhone users who haven't updated their OS. The underlying chain — six vulnerabilities across iOS 18.4-18.7 — was first documented by Google's Threat Intelligence Group in March 2026, describing activity dating to at least November 2025; Google reported the flaws to Apple in late 2025, and all were patched by the release of iOS 26.3 (most earlier). Security researchers cited by CertiK and Phemex say the exploit kit has cumulatively drained **$5.57 million from more than 20 wallets**, naming MetaMask, Trust Wallet, Phantom, Exodus and Coinbase as targeted apps; the time period over which this total accumulated is not specified in available reporting. Google observed campaigns against targets in Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Malaysia and Ukraine involving commercial surveillance vendors and suspected state-sponsored actors — no attribution specific to the crypto-theft cases. CZ (Binance) publicly amplified the warning, noting wallet apps cannot patch the underlying OS on a user's behalf. Mitigation is simple and complete: update to a current iOS version.

**CryptoPayment Gateway WordPress plugin — CVE-2026-81648, CVSS 10.0, no patch:** Unchanged from prior editions. No known fix exists; plugin remains removed from the WordPress Plugin Directory. Mitigation: remove the plugin, block the endpoint at the WAF layer, rotate any stored wallet credentials.

**Solana validator Linux kernel flaw — CVE-2026-98432, CVSS 9.8, Ubuntu still unpatched:** Unchanged from prior editions. AWS has shipped patches for Amazon Linux 2/2023; Ubuntu 24.04/22.04/26.10/26.04 remain vulnerable. Interim mitigations: block inbound TCP packets carrying IPv4 options, disable MPTCP and TCP request-socket migration.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Legacy/deprecated systems still live on crypto-adjacent platforms (Gnosis Pay, this window)**

Gnosis Pay's breach hit a system the company had already scheduled for retirement — a reminder that "we're sunsetting this" is not the same as "this is no longer attack surface." For bounty/audit work on any exchange, card issuer, or wallet provider: ask specifically what legacy or soon-to-be-deprecated components are still live, what credentials and customer data they still hold, and whether they receive the same patch/monitoring cadence as production systems.

**2. Multi-week undetected attacker dwell time before exploit, as a pattern not an outlier (SEAL/Binance, this window)**

SEAL's report puts real numbers on something worth internalizing: TraderTraitor-style campaigns at Binance, Bitget, LayerZero (Kelp DAO) and AFX all show three-to-eight-week gaps between initial compromise and the visible exploit. If you're doing incident response or a security review, don't assume a clean bill of health just because nothing's moved yet — check for dormant footholds (stale credentials, unused deployment tokens, unreviewed AI-tool integrations) that an attacker may already hold.

**3. Fuzz-testing of legacy/fallback code paths in execution clients (Reth, this window)**

The Reth state-root bug was found by fuzzing a legacy calculation path that only activates under specific hardware or chain conditions (lower-end hardware, OP Stack chains) — exactly the kind of rarely-exercised branch that manual review tends to skip. If you're auditing an L2 or any client with multiple state-calculation code paths (legacy vs. current, fast vs. slow), prioritize fuzzing the path that's least exercised in normal operation, not the one everyone already knows to check.

**4. Zero-click mobile exploit chains as a wallet-security blind spot (DarkSword, this window)**

DarkSword is a reminder that "the wallet app is secure" says nothing about the OS underneath it — a six-bug Safari chain can exfiltrate seed phrases with zero user interaction. For wallet-app security reviews, don't stop at the application layer: document and communicate OS-version risk to users explicitly (in-app warnings for outdated iOS/Android versions are a real, implementable mitigation most wallet apps still don't ship).

**5. Precautionary-shutdown-after-threat as a distinct incident class worth tracking (GOPAX, this window)**

GOPAX's suspension was triggered by a threat email with no confirmed breach — a pattern distinct from a confirmed exploit but operationally identical in its effects (frozen withdrawals, customer panic, reputational risk). If you track exchange incidents for research or bounty-surface mapping, these precautionary events are worth logging separately from confirmed breaches, since they reveal how an exchange actually responds under pressure before any real compromise is proven.
