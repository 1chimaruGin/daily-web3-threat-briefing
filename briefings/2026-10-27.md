### Bloom (Solana Telegram Trading Bot) — 49 Wallets Drained via Validly-Signed Transactions, Root Cause Disputed

**Chain:** Solana.
**Status:** Active/contained. Occurred October 26, 2026.
**Impact:** ~9,408 SOL (~$947,000-954,000; two sources differ slightly on the SOL/USD conversion used).
**What happened:** Bloom, a Telegram-based trading bot supporting Solana and EVM networks, had 49 wallets belonging to 9 users drained in a tightly coordinated sweep. On-chain monitor Onchain Lens flagged the outflows converging on a single receiving address at 04:26 UTC. Blockchain analytics firm Bitquery traced the drain (48 of the 49 wallets in its own count) completing in roughly 94 seconds, and found that every transfer was validly signed by the source wallet's own key — some of the drained wallets had been inactive for months, and the order of withdrawals suggests targets were organized before the attack began. Bloom paused some services, advised users to migrate remaining funds to freshly generated wallets, and pledged full reimbursement to all nine affected users.
**Root cause:** Disputed/unresolved. Bloom's own account attributes the breach to a remote-code-execution vulnerability in a third-party software module and states "no private keys were compromised." That claim is not yet reconciled with Bitquery's finding that every drained transaction carried a valid signature from the wallet's own key — if no key material was exposed, the mechanism by which the attacker obtained signing authority is unexplained. Treat Bloom's RCE/no-key-exposure framing as the company's stated position, not an independently confirmed root cause, pending its promised forensic report.
**Bug class:** key-management / signing-authority-compromise-without-confirmed-key-exposure — an attacker produced cryptographically valid signatures from 49 distinct wallets without (on the company's account) obtaining the underlying private keys, a gap the available on-chain evidence does not yet explain.

### Yapeswap — Zero-Quorum Governance Flaw Lets 0.000055% Voting Power Seize Fee-Setter Role

**Chain:** Ethereum.
**Status:** Closed, one-off incident. Occurred October 25, 2026, ~02:15-02:19 UTC.
**Impact:** ~$21,900 (not disputed, but small).
**What happened:** Yapeswap is a small Uniswap-V2-style DEX whose governance contract, "WorkersUnion," required zero quorum to pass a proposal. The attacker staged the attack over several days: swapping ETH for the project's VISION token on October 16, locking and delegating it, then submitting a proposal to call `setFeeToSetter` on the YapeFactory contract. Because WorkersUnion imposed no minimum participation threshold, the proposal passed on October 25 backed by only about 0.000055% of total voting power, handing the attacker the factory's fee-setter permission. The attacker then deployed a malicious Registry contract that returned a forged address from `latestVault()` while `vaults()` still listed the legitimate vault — a split response that caused YapePair to count transferred user deposits as farm assets without minting any redeemable shares against them, producing the fund loss.
**Root cause:** A governance mechanism with no quorum requirement, combined with a factory/registry trust assumption that didn't cross-check `latestVault()` against `vaults()` before treating a deposit as backed.
**Bug class:** access-control / zero-quorum-governance-takeover, paired with accounting / vault-registry-address-spoofing.

### Keystone — Website Breach Exposes Customer Data and a Device Authentication Key

**Chain:** N/A — hardware wallet manufacturer, affects device supply-chain trust rather than any specific chain.
**Status:** Ongoing remediation. Intrusion detected October 20, 2026; confirmed October 23; publicly disclosed October 25.
**Impact:** $0 in funds, seed phrases, or private keys confirmed stolen. ~25,964 customer order/contact records and a device WebAuthn authentication key exposed; 18,835 unsold inventory units are being destroyed as a precaution.
**What happened:** Keystone said a third party gained unauthorized access to its Shopify-powered online store, which it detected on October 20 and confirmed, after reviewing API activity with Shopify, had resulted in theft of customer data by October 23. The exposed data includes names, contact details, shipping addresses, and order history — not payment-card or bank data, and not recovery phrases, private keys, or wallet passwords. Critically, the exposure also included a device WebAuthn authentication key used to verify that a Keystone hardware wallet is genuine; a stolen authenticity key raises counterfeit/tampered-device risk distinct from the data theft itself. Keystone has not disclosed how the attacker gained initial access, how many customers in total were affected, or whether it has identified the attacker. Several customers have publicly claimed they were contacted about orders placed well beyond Keystone's advertised 180-day data-deletion window — one citing an order over two years old — and one reported a scam phone call spoofing a Montreal police number that referenced a pre-breach order, which The Block says it could not independently verify. Separately, a phishing campaign impersonating a different hardware-wallet brand, Foundation, sent "recall" emails this window apparently exploiting the general breach anxiety; Foundation has stated this is purely social engineering with no underlying infrastructure or firmware flaw, so it is not counted here as a vulnerability.
**Root cause:** A vulnerability in a third-party data-monitoring tool connected to Keystone's storefront allowed privilege escalation and exfiltration; Keystone has blocked access and patched the underlying flaw but has not published a full technical write-up.
**Bug class:** supply-chain / third-party-monitoring-tool-privilege-escalation — a vendor-side tool integrated with the storefront was compromised and used to reach customer data and a device-authenticity credential, independent of any wallet firmware or on-chain flaw.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. "No private keys exposed" claims that don't survive a valid-signature trace (Bloom, new this window)**

Bloom's defense — a third-party RCE, no key material taken — is directly undercut by Bitquery's own finding that every drained transfer carried a valid signature from the source wallet. Any time a service claims "no keys exposed" after an incident involving dozens of distinctly-keyed wallets moving funds in near-simultaneous, correctly-signed transactions, the claim itself is a hypothesis worth probing: look for session-hijacking, signing-oracle abuse, or an RPC/relayer the bot trusted to co-sign on the user's behalf — all of which would produce valid signatures without ever exposing a raw private key, and would explain the gap Bloom hasn't yet closed.

**2. Zero-quorum or low-quorum governance as a standing, recurring pattern (Yapeswap, new this window; X100, Arkadiko, standing)**

Yapeswap is now the third governance-takeover incident in this series' recent window (after X100 and Arkadiko), and the cheapest: ~0.000055% of voting power was enough because the contract enforced no minimum quorum at all. For any protocol with on-chain governance controlling a factory, fee-setter, or registry role: check not just whether quorum exists, but whether it's enforced as a hard floor independent of total-supply fluctuations, and whether the role being granted (here, `feeToSetter`) can itself deploy or point to new trusted contracts (here, a spoofable `Registry`).

**3. Split-view registry/vault lookups that don't cross-validate (Yapeswap, new this window)**

The forged-vault mechanic — `latestVault()` returning one address while `vaults()` still lists the legitimate one — is a narrow but checkable pattern: any contract that exposes two different read paths to "the current trusted vault/pool/oracle" is vulnerable if downstream logic trusts whichever one it happens to call without reconciling the two. Worth sweeping for this exact shape in any registry-pattern contract paired with a role (like fee-setter) capable of redeploying the registry itself.

**4. Hardware-wallet vendor storefronts and third-party integrations as the real attack surface (Keystone, new this window)**

Keystone's breach, like the Trezor/ShipMonk and SafePal incidents referenced in its own coverage, didn't touch firmware or seed-generation at all — it went through a third-party tool integrated with the vendor's Shopify storefront. For any hardware-wallet or custody vendor: the device's cryptography can be flawless while the surrounding e-commerce/CRM/data-monitoring stack remains the softest target, and a stolen device-authenticity key (as opposed to a stolen seed) creates a slower-burning counterfeit-supply-chain risk rather than an immediate fund loss — worth distinguishing the two when triaging severity.

**5. Read the on-chain trail, not the first statement — still the dominant theme (standing, reinforced)**

This window adds confirmation rather than a new case: CoinDCX's CEO call of "false alarm" and its spokesperson's "unused since January 2025" claim are now both directly quoted and both squarely contradicted by ZachXBT's independently-sourced sweep evidence in the very same article reporting them. Initia's and X100's updates this window are both forensic/regulatory follow-through (Korean exchange flags, Tornado Cash trace) rather than new facts about the exploits themselves — a reminder that between an incident's first statement and its eventual on-chain-verified account, the gap is often where the real story sits.
