### Avalanche Tranche Vault (Fence Finance / Valinor Digital) — ~$1.01M Flash-Loan Accounting Exploit

**Chain:** Avalanche.
**Status:** Active/ongoing per the detecting security firm; no public statement from Fence or Valinor Digital found as of writing; no recovery reported.
**Impact:** Approximately $1.01 million in USDC, described by security firm Blockaid as the loss "so far," spread across roughly two transactions.
**What happened:** On the evening of Tuesday, October 27 (ET), an attacker used a flash loan to fund a deposit/withdrawal sequence against a "Tranche Vault" on Avalanche that Blockaid identified as "related to" Valinor Digital and Fence. The vault transferred 1,013,353.61 USDC to the attacker's contract at 8:00pm ET; Blockaid posted its alert at 9:07pm ET the same evening, calling the exploit "ongoing."
**Root cause:** The vault's settlement logic calculated available withdrawal capacity by subtracting outstanding principal from funded principal. At a UTC day rollover, this logic incorrectly treated a scheduled principal repayment as already received — even though no USDC had actually been repaid — which reduced the recorded outstanding principal and reopened roughly 1.013 million USDC of withdrawal capacity that had already been accounted for. The attacker, using flash-loan liquidity to fund the initial deposit/withdrawal sequence, resubmitted the same withdrawal parameters a second time and extracted that reopened capacity. Blockaid's own framing describes this as the attacker using the flash loan to "mint inflated tranche shares" and then redeeming those shares for the vault's remaining USDC — consistent with the same underlying flaw (the contract failing to distinguish a scheduled repayment from funds actually received).
**Bug class:** accounting-logic / stale-principal-settlement enabling double-withdrawal, funded by a flash loan rather than requiring attacker capital.

### Udesk Third-Party Chat-Widget Script — Live Deposit-Address Hijack + Clipboard Hijack + Credential Theft on Multiple Exchanges

**Chain:** Multi-chain exchange infrastructure (addresses spoofed cover Bitcoin, EVM-compatible chains, TRON, Solana, Litecoin, and Dogecoin).
**Status:** Disclosed October 27, 2026 by security firm SlowMist. No confirmation of full remediation; SlowMist's advisory implies the compromise may still be partially live on affected sites.
**Impact:** No confirmed aggregate theft figure. The address-swap module was confirmed active on three named exchanges — MGBX, Biking Exchange, and BitxEX — while the broader credential-harvesting payload runs on any site that loads the compromised script.
**What happened:** SlowMist found that Udesk's customer-service chat-widget script had a hidden loader appended to it, contained in a file named `udeskApi.2026-10-16.cached.js` — the embedded date suggests the compromise may have been live for at least 11 days before disclosure. The loader hex-encoded its target URL to evade naive scanning, then fetched a second payload, `deeps.js`, from a typosquatted domain, `udeskqlobal[.]com` (a "q" substituted for "g" in "udeskglobal"). On any site embedding the script, the payload read cookies and browser storage, captured form input, and intercepted request bodies/URLs containing strings like "login," "signup," or "password," exfiltrating the data to the same malicious domain. Loading the widget inside an iframe provided no protection, since scripts loaded into the host page were not sandboxed. A second, narrower module activated only for MGBX, Biking Exchange, and BitxEX: on their deposit pages, it polled every 200 milliseconds and swapped the displayed deposit address for one of six attacker-controlled wallets, while separately hijacking clipboard-copy behavior through three distinct code paths — meaning a user who copies an address and later pastes it elsewhere may still see the tampered value, defeating the common safeguard of re-checking a copied address against the original page.
**Root cause:** Third-party customer-service widget supply-chain compromise. SlowMist has not disclosed how the malicious code entered Udesk's script pipeline, how many sites load the affected script, or whether any funds were actually sent to the substitute addresses. SlowMist warned that merely removing the script tag does not revoke hooks and timers already installed in a page, and urged affected sites to rotate credentials from a clean environment.
**Bug class:** supply-chain / third-party-widget-script-compromise — combining broad credential/session theft with targeted deposit-address spoofing and clipboard hijacking.

## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

### Malicious Firefox Extensions Impersonate Phantom, Jupiter, and Bitget — Disclosed and Removed

Security firm Socket disclosed on October 27 that three malicious Firefox extensions used Unicode-homoglyph branding to impersonate the Phantom, Jupiter, and Bitget wallets on Mozilla's official Add-ons store, each published from a separate throwaway developer account. All three steal BIP-39 seed phrases and private keys (in both EVM-hex and Solana base58 formats) by reading form inputs, listening for paste/focusout events, and scanning page text for recovery-phrase grids, routing stolen secrets to brand-specific subdomains of a single C2 apex, `bonto[.]io`. The Phantom variant goes further: it injects into the page's main world to hook Phantom's own wallet provider, fetches a remote, toggleable payload specifically built to stay inert during Mozilla's review and activate only after approval, and bundles a complete Node.js drainer backend that automatically sweeps funds to hardcoded payout addresses across nine chains (Ethereum, BSC, Polygon, Arbitrum, Optimism, Base, Avalanche, Solana, Bitcoin), logging secrets to a Telegram bot. Socket also found an unused `CLAUDE.md` file bundled in the Phantom and Jupiter packages containing what it identifies as an AI-assistant jailbreak prompt — never loaded at runtime, but suggestive of AI-assisted malware authoring and an attempt to subvert AI-assisted extension review. All three listings were live during Socket's analysis and had been removed from the Firefox Add-ons store by time of publication.

### South Korean Financial-Sector Breach Wave Reaches Bithumb

Bithumb confirmed on October 28 that an outside party gained unauthorized access to its internal staff mobile groupware system, exposing employee (and possibly partner-company) personal information. Bithumb and Korea's Financial Services Commission both state the compromised system is fully separated from the exchange's trading and custody infrastructure, and that no leak of payment- or wallet-transfer-usable data — including wallet addresses — has been confirmed; whether customer data was also exposed remains under review. ## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Settlement logic that conflates "scheduled" with "received" (Avalanche Tranche Vault, new this window)**

The core bug here is simple once named: a vault's accounting treated a principal repayment that was merely *scheduled* at a UTC day rollover as if it had *actually arrived*, reopening withdrawal capacity that should have stayed closed. For any tranche/vault/lending contract with time-based settlement, checkpoints, or accrual logic, check every place state advances on a clock or epoch boundary rather than on a confirmed, asset-transferring event — and check whether that state can be read or acted on twice before the real transfer happens. The flash loan here wasn't the vulnerability; it was just cheap capital to exploit a bug that didn't require any capital to begin with.

**2. Privileged/team-fund withdrawal paths with no public explanation (Harvest Moon/NEAR, new this window)**

Harvest Moon's team-fund contract let an attacker withdraw 1.04M GEAR and 25K USDC, and as of this writing nobody has said how. That gap is itself the lead: any contract with a team-, treasury-, or admin-controlled balance is worth checking for the same three usual suspects — an under-scoped access-control check, a key that's reachable through a dependency the team doesn't directly control, or a withdrawal function that trusts caller-supplied state it shouldn't. Until Near Tinker Union's post-mortem lands, treat "team funds drained, mechanism unknown" as an open, unresolved bug class rather than a solved incident.

**3. Third-party widgets and SDKs as a deposit-address attack surface (Udesk/SlowMist, new this window)**

This is the second time in a week this series has tracked a third-party web component (a documentation site for Predict.fun, now a customer-service chat widget for at least three exchanges) sitting in a position to tamper with what a user sees or copies. For any platform that embeds third-party chat, analytics, or support widgets on pages where users view or copy deposit/withdrawal addresses: check whether that widget's script is pinned/subresource-integrity-checked, whether it runs with access to the host page's DOM (iframe embedding is not a safety guarantee, as this incident shows), and whether the page re-validates a displayed address against a trusted backend value immediately before a user acts on it — not just immediately before display. Clipboard-hijacking specifically defeats the common manual mitigation of "copy, then re-check," so any mitigation guidance that stops at that step is incomplete.

**4. Validly-signed transfers that keep happening after a company says it's "investigating" (Predict.fun, standing, reinforced)**

Predict.fun's own disclosure didn't stop the attacker — a transfer went out about an hour after the company's public statement, and the full timeline now shows the compromise had been running for five days before anyone said anything publicly. For wallet/key-compromise incidents generally, "we are investigating" and "the affected system has been taken offline" are not the same as "the attacker's access has been revoked." Treat ongoing wallet activity, not the disclosure timestamp, as the real signal for when a compromise is actually contained.

**5. Mass, fast, multi-wallet drains with no stated mechanism are still a live pattern worth tracking (Scam Sniffer incident, new this window)**

A same-pattern drain — rapid, many unrelated wallets, single consolidation address, immediate conversion to a liquid asset — recurs often enough that it's worth treating as its own category distinct from single-protocol exploits. These are usually downstream of phishing, drainer-kits, or batched approval abuse rather than a new smart-contract bug, but when the mechanism isn't named, it's worth checking whether any of the hit wallets share a common dApp, wallet-connect integration, or recently-granted token approval — that correlation is often what eventually identifies the vector.
