### Initia L1 — Unbacked Multi-Token Minting Drains Three Bridges, Chain Halted (Day 2, Figures Disputed/Unconfirmed)

**Chain:** Initia (Move-based L1 for interconnected rollups), bridged via LayerZero to Ethereum, via Noble/CCTP for USDC, with attacker prep activity on Osmosis.
**Status:** New. Initia halted its L1 and all rollups at **15:41:30 UTC on October 23, 2026** following a security incident. Security researcher Spreek raised the first public alarm at 15:27 UTC: "Initia chain hacked and a bunch of tokens infiniminted. Hacker has cashed out $2.6m thus far with more in flight." Initia's own statement did not establish an amount lost or describe the vulnerability, saying only it would "share verified information through our official channels as it becomes available," and asked users to be wary of unofficial recovery offers. As of the most recent reporting checked, the chain remains halted with no resumption timeline, and Initia has not publicly confirmed losses or root cause.
**What happened:** Per Bitquery's reconciliation (the most granular account found), the attacker spent over six hours prepping — funding an Ethereum wallet via Tornado Cash, running small test transfers through Osmosis and a test LayerZero message to Initia, then acquiring INIT gas — before uploading a small program to Initia at 14:59:17 UTC and running it four times in 13 seconds, each run minting a fresh batch of tokens backed by nothing: 3M iUSD, 2M AUSD0, 2M USDC-equivalent, and 200 ETH-equivalent, a gross face value of **~$7.48M**. The attacker then tried 11 withdrawals in 33 minutes; only four actually reached Ethereum as real bridged-out assets — 1.4M AUSD0, 1M USDC, 105 ETH, and 80K USDC, a realized total Bitquery puts at **~$2.73M** (consistent with, and a later refinement of, Spreek's initial $2.6M alert) — because the three bridges' reserves ran out or rejected the fakes: Noble's USDC reserve collapsed to $63 before the attacker could drain more, and a final 200,000 AUSD0 message was blocked at 17:51 UTC when four of five LayerZero DVN verifiers signed off but LayerZero Labs' own verifier did not. The remaining **~$4.55M** of printed face value — mostly worthless iUSD, since only 1.69M real iUSD exists — never left Initia. The 1,135.5 ETH (~$2.73M) that did reach Ethereum was consolidated into a single wallet five seconds after the chain halted and sat unmoved as of the last check. Third-party analytics firm D2 Finance attributed the exploit to "a vulnerability in a Move function," but **Initia itself has not confirmed this or any other root-cause mechanism** — Bitquery states plainly: "Initia has not said how the program got past the chain's rules." Stablecoin issuer Agora separately disputed that AUSD has any smart contracts deployed on Initia at all.
**Bug class:** logic-error / unbacked-token-minting + bridge-trust-asymmetry — a chain-level program was able to mint tokens recognized by cross-chain bridges as genuine, and the bridges paid out real reserve assets against them without any on-chain backing check.

### Arkadiko (Stacks) — DIKO Governance Takeover Drains Vault Collateral, USDA Depegs (Day 3, First Appearance in This Series)

**Chain:** Stacks, with stolen value moved out via sBTC peg-outs to Bitcoin and USDCx burns to Ethereum USDC.
**Status:** New to this briefing series — the attack occurred and was disclosed on October 22, 2026, before the previous edition's cutoff, but was not caught in prior research; included now as the first appearance. Per Bitquery's investigation (dated Oct 22, on-chain figures verified Oct 23): a single wallet bought enough of Arkadiko's DIKO governance token to pass "proposal 12" entirely on its own, with zero votes against, at a cost of roughly **$51,000** in DIKO purchases (77% of the STX used to buy those votes traced back to a prior, unrelated StackSwap heist). The proposal installed a contract with two innocuously-named, unchecked functions: "claim-pending-yield" (drains any token from the vault pool to any address) and "settle-fee-budget" (mints unlimited USDA to any address). The vote closed at 16:04 UTC; sBTC was taken at 16:06, stSTX plus a large USDA mint followed at 16:09. Arkadiko disabled the malicious contract by 18:08, paused governance by 18:14, and had the DAO burn most of the printed USDA by 19:21 — roughly three hours from exploit to containment. Arkadiko's loss: **2,065,481 stSTX (~$780K)**, **1.6522 sBTC (~$135K)**, and **~$173K of STX** bought with the printed USDA before most of it was burned — a total of **~$1.09M**. The same governance-takeover technique (DIKO/STX vote-buying to pass a self-serving proposal) separately hit Stacks DEX StackSwap twice — once via an unrelated wallet that had seized a role back in July (pulling ~$31K on Oct 20), and once by the Arkadiko attacker winning a StackSwap proposal by a 0.19% margin roughly 15 hours after the Arkadiko hit (draining 49 pools for ~$39K) — bringing the combined Arkadiko+StackSwap total to **~$1.16M** per Bitquery. Despite the fast containment, **USDA lost its dollar peg and Arkadiko's TVL fell approximately 90% in a day.** Proceeds moved via sBTC peg-outs to Bitcoin (~$1.02M, 4.86 BTC), USDCx burns to Ethereum USDC (~$305K), and STX routed through swap services including ChangeNOW (~$320K); 213.3 ETH was sent into Tornado Cash the morning of Oct 23. A secondary report cited within Bitquery's writeup states Arkadiko asked exchanges to freeze stolen funds and filed a police report, though this detail is attributed to a third-party source rather than confirmed directly from Arkadiko's own channel.
**Bug class:** access-control / governance-takeover — Arkadiko's DAO allowed a single wallet with sufficient capital to buy enough of a thinly-held governance token to unilaterally pass a proposal installing unchecked, unrestricted vault-draining and minting functions, with no timelock, multisig veto, or quorum-diversity requirement that this specific attack had to clear.

### 79thVault / 79AU — Bitquery Deep-Dive Revises Loss to $14.35M, Finds the Exposure Was Never Fully Closed (Day 18)

**Chain:** BNB Chain (79AU/USDT pool on PancakeSwap).
**Status:** Major update. Bitquery's investigation (dated/verified Oct 8, surfaced in this window's research) revises the commonly-reported ~$12.5M loss upward to **$14.35M**, driven by a second seller wallet that sold later the same day (Oct 7) and was missed by initial reporting — 2.52 million 79AU pulled across 9 transactions (vs. "7" first reported), for a combined $14.35M in USDT extracted (Seller A: $12.60M over roughly an hour; Seller B: $1.75M about five hours later).
**What happened / root cause:** The 79AU token contract has a privileged "pull" function — not a secret bug, but a feature the project had already used 251,986 times since June 10 to fund staking rewards (moving ~5.1M 79AU over four months into its own reward contracts). Two wallets held this permission: the deployer wallet (since launch) and a hot wallet (granted the day after launch) that an automated bot used for routine daily pulls. On the night of Oct 6-7 the bot went silent for nearly 6 hours — its longest gap ever — then on waking, after 420 routine pulls, began sending pulls to a brand-new wallet instead of the project's own contracts, using gas parameters it had never used in 350,000+ prior transactions. The pulled tokens were then sold back into the same pool for real USDT, collapsing the price (one illustrative pair of pulls four seconds apart tripled the quoted price purely by shrinking the token side of the pool with no USDT added). **The exposure was never fully closed**: the project's multisig revoked only the compromised hot wallet's pull permission at 08:24 UTC (about an hour after the first pull, and after Seller A had already finished) — the deployer wallet retained the identical permission throughout, and within an hour of the thief's on-chain ransom message (demanding to keep 25% as a "white-hat" bounty, against the team's offered 10%), the multisig granted the same pull permission to a brand-new wallet across all eight related contracts. Bitquery's own read-only check found both the deployer wallet and the new wallet could still pull roughly 900,000 79AU — about 95% of what remains in the pool — at will, with nothing paid in, as of its last check. No funds had been returned. Proceeds (~$13.6M in BNB) sit across three wallets; a smaller slice (~$360K) was routed through a cross-chain swap service and has likely left BNB Chain.
**Bug class:** access-control / privileged-admin-function-abuse — a token contract shipped with an unrestricted mint/pull-style function used routinely for legitimate operations, and the incident response removed the permission from one compromised holder without revoking it from the system as a whole.

## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

### DarkSword — Weaponized iOS Exploit Framework Targeting Crypto Wallet Keys (New)

SlowMist founder Cosine warned on Oct 24 that iPhone/iPad users should update promptly against "DarkSword," an attack tool that, per SlowMist's analysis, can bypass iOS app-sandbox restrictions once triggered via a malicious link opened in Safari, then steal Keychain-stored passwords plus local private-key files for crypto wallets and other sensitive data. The framework reportedly evolved after leaking in March 2026, with a "DarkSword-PROV2" variant surfacing on GitHub by July, and is described as a highly automated end-to-end pipeline integrating vulnerability delivery, device/data collection, exfiltration, and crypto-wallet fund aggregation — affecting iOS versions 18.6-18.7.2 and 26-26.1.
**Bug class:** mobile-OS-exploit / wallet-keychain-theft.

### LiteLLM — Second Supply-Chain Compromise, Credential Harvester Explicitly Targets Crypto Wallets (New)

On Oct 21, 2026, attackers published a malicious release (v1.107.0) of the LiteLLM LLM-gateway package to PyPI and Docker, live for roughly two hours before removal. The injected code auto-executes on Python startup and harvests over 50 categories of secrets — explicitly including cryptocurrency wallets alongside SSH keys, cloud-provider credentials (AWS/GCP/Azure), Kubernetes/Docker/npm/Vault tokens, and CI/CD secrets — and supports remote code execution. Per reporting, the attacker's access chain traced back to a configuration file accidentally committed to a public repository in May 2026 containing a master key to a LiteLLM Agent Platform deployment, used in October to extract a GitHub CLI OAuth token granting owner-level access across the organization's repositories. This is the project's second supply-chain compromise this year. Worth flagging alongside Railway's own unconfirmed claim that its breach "may be connected to a series of attacks against platform companies intended to compromise AI inference providers" — LiteLLM is itself an AI-inference-adjacent gateway, and both incidents independently show dev-infrastructure compromises reaching stored crypto private keys as a side effect of targeting AI/cloud tooling, not wallets directly.
**Bug class:** key-management / supply-chain.

### EIP-7702 Delegation Drainers — Persistent Pattern, Not Newly Dated to This Window

Not a new event this window, but still a live pattern: EIP-7702 ("Pectra") delegation authorizations can sit dormant before an attacker activates them via a sponsored (gas-free) transaction that drains the account atomically. Wintermute's research found more than 97% of malicious EIP-7702 delegations trace to a single reusable sweeper contract ("CrimeEnjoyor"). No new individually-reported case surfaced this window.
**Bug class:** signature-hygiene / delegation-authorization-abuse.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Bridges that trust a chain's own mint event instead of verifying real collateral (Initia pattern, new this window)**

Initia's three bridges (two LayerZero-based, one Noble/CCTP) each paid out real reserve assets because a fake token minted on Initia *looked* like a genuine locked-and-mirrored balance. For any lock-and-mint or burn-and-mint bridge audit: ask what actually stops a chain-level mint with no real backing from being treated as redeemable on the other side — a bridge adapter should verify backing independently, not just confirm "a burn/mint event occurred."

**2. Governance takeovers of low-liquidity or concentrated tokens (Arkadiko pattern, new this window)**

A single ~$51K DIKO purchase was enough to pass a proposal unopposed and install unrestricted vault-drain and unlimited-mint functions. For any protocol with on-chain DAO-controlled upgrades: check the cost to acquire a passing/quorum-busting share of the governance token at current liquidity, whether a timelock or multisig veto exists between proposal passage and execution, and whether newly-installed contract functions get any independent caller/authorization check — "it passed governance" should not be the only gate on a function that can move user funds.

**3. Privileged functions reused for "routine ops" that were never meant to be a kill switch (79thVault reinforcement, this window)**

79AU's pull function was used safely 251,986 times before one misuse turned it into a $14.35M drain — and the incident response proved the team didn't fully understand their own exposure, since the deployer wallet and a freshly-granted wallet retained the same unrestricted power afterward. When reviewing any token or pool contract with an "admin pulls liquidity for rewards" pattern, map every address that holds the permission, not just the one most recently compromised, and verify revocation removes the capability from the system, not just from one key.

**4. Dev-infrastructure and AI-tooling supply chain as a path to wallet keys (reinforced this window)**

Railway's breach and LiteLLM's second supply-chain compromise both reached stored private keys as a side effect of attacks aimed at broader dev/cloud/AI infrastructure, not at crypto specifically. For wallet, bot, or treasury-ops security reviews: map every CI/CD secret store, PaaS environment variable, and AI-tooling dependency that could reach a signing key, and treat "this package/platform isn't crypto-specific" as irrelevant to the actual blast radius.

**5. Post-mortem and reconciliation latency as its own signal (standing, reinforced this window)**

CoinDCX's own explanation still doesn't address ZachXBT's specific ten-month-sweep objection; Initia hasn't disclosed how its minting bypass actually worked; and 79thVault's team re-granted the exact permission that caused the drain within an hour of a ransom demand. An official statement closing a story is not the same as the story being technically resolved — read the on-chain reconciliation (Bitquery, ZachXBT, independent trackers), not just the press statement.
