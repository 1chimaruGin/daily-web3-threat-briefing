### X100 (Ethereum) — Flash-Loan-Funded Governance Senate Takeover Drains $4.85M Treasury (Day 1)

**Chain:** Ethereum.
**Status:** New. Occurred Saturday, October 24, 2026. Security firm Blockaid detected and warned the community as the attack unfolded, around 10:49 a.m. ET. As of the last check (roughly 3 p.m. ET the same day), the incident "remains ongoing," with further operations reported targeting additional Uniswap V3 liquidity positions and roughly 12.2 million X tokens still sitting in the project's "Freezer" treasury. No official X100/X 100 Global statement had been issued at time of writing.
**What happened:** X100's governance uses a three-seat "Senate" structure. The attacker borrowed approximately 1.94 million USDT through an Aave flash loan, used it to purchase X tokens, and registered helper contracts to occupy two of the three Senate seats — a voting majority obtained and spent within the same operation. Per security firm Exvul (quoted in reporting), "Governance lacked voting snapshots, minimum holding periods and execution delays," which let the attacker "acquire voting power, approve malicious proposals and execute transactions almost immediately." With majority control, the attacker passed proposals that sold treasury X tokens and drained liquidity pools. Blockaid traced roughly $4.85 million in USDT proceeds plus approximately 550,000 X tokens extracted across three transactions (a second source gives $4.7M instead of $4.85M — the discrepancy is unresolved and reported here as disputed). One transaction moved 400,000 X tokens from the project's Freezer treasury into an attacker-controlled liquidity pool, returning only about 36 USDT, before the tokens were sold through a legitimate Uniswap V3 pool. The X token price collapsed from the $50-60 range to approximately $1.75 — a decline exceeding 96% — with DEX liquidity falling to just a few dollars.
**Bug class:** access-control / governance-takeover-via-flash-loan — a flash loan supplied the capital to buy a controlling share of a thinly-held governance token instantaneously, and the governance system had no voting snapshot, minimum holding period, or execution delay to prevent a same-transaction capture-and-spend.

### Initia L1 — Team Discloses MoveVM Script-Caching Root Cause, Chain Still Halted (Day 3)

**Chain:** Initia (Move-based L1 for interconnected rollups), bridged via LayerZero to Ethereum, via Noble/CCTP for USDC.
**Status:** Update. Initia's L1 and rollups remain paused following the October 23 unauthorized multi-token minting incident (see prior editions for the full attack timeline and the disputed $2.7M-realized / ~$7.48M-gross-mint figures). The material development this window: Initia itself — not a third-party analytics firm — has now published a root-cause explanation.
**What happened:** Per Initia's own disclosure (via X, repeated in multiple outlets Oct 24), the attacker exploited "a script caching vulnerability in Initia MoveVM" that let them "bypass security checks and forge MoveVM state," crediting themselves with WETH, iUSD, and USDC balances "without actual backing." Critically, the articles describing Initia's statement specify that "the total supply of these tokens remained unchanged" — meaning the exploit forged a credited balance without a matching change to the recorded total-supply counter, an accounting desync between what the chain believed an account held and what was actually backed, rather than a straightforward inflation of a mint counter. INIT itself was unaffected; its total supply was not touched. The confirmed realized-theft figure remains $2.7M (105 WETH, ~1.4M AUSD0, ~1.08M USDC, swapped into 1,135.52 ETH), unrevised by this disclosure. Initia says security patches are "expected to be completed and audited within several days." No restart date has been given; both L1 and rollups remain paused.
**Bug class:** logic-error / VM-state-forgery-via-script-caching — a caching layer inside the MoveVM let an attacker bypass balance-validation checks and write a forged account state, decoupling a credited token balance from the ledger's actual backing/supply accounting.

## 3. 🟢 Advisories & patches

### Swell (swETH/rswETH) — DAO Hot-Key/Oracle-Bot Compromise, Contained Before Fund Loss (New)

Security researcher Spreek reported that an unknown actor had control of Swell DAO's operational hot keys — including the oracle bot that sets the swETH/rswETH exchange rates — as of 14:25 UTC on October 21, 2026. The actor raised both tokens' exchange rates at the maximum the system allows, 1% per hour, with no new ETH backing the increases (by Oct 22, swETH had climbed about 3.0% above its legitimate rate and rswETH about 2.0%). Four malicious upgrade contracts were deployed that, if executed through Swell's timelock, could have swept approximately 2,513 ETH (~$6M). None of the four were fired. Swell confirmed no ETH had been deposited into or withdrawn from the affected contracts and that the protocol remained fully operational while it investigated. Researchers raised an unconfirmed possible tie to Railway, the cloud deployment platform whose Oct 21 security disclosure (already a standing incident in this briefing series) described an "unauthorized third party" potentially reading customers' decrypted environment variables — Swell itself has not attributed the compromise to Railway. No further resolution update (key rotation confirmation, etc.) was found beyond the initial Oct 22 reporting.
**Bug class:** key-management / hot-key-and-oracle-compromise — DAO-operated signing keys for a price-setting oracle bot were compromised, allowing unbacked rate inflation and staged (but unexecuted) malicious contract upgrades.

### Balancer V1 / Aave — Blockaid Flags a Balance-Snapshot Monitoring Blind Spot (Retrospective, Not a New Exploit)

Blockaid published an October 23 analysis (not describing a new incident) of an August 30-31, 2026 Balancer V1 exploit episode. The attacker routed more than $1 billion in flash-loan assets through three Aave V3 markets as collateral, borrowed a further $788 million against those positions, and used the capital to exploit Balancer V1's legacy, unmaintained, pause-mechanism-less code for a loss Blockaid puts at roughly $250,000 (a separate episode-wide tally of ~$1.39M across 120 pools from related copycat activity covers a different scope). Aave itself took no loss, because the attacker's collateral positions opened and closed within the same transactions, leaving the account with "effectively no balance" immediately before or after — exactly the pattern that periodic balance-snapshot monitoring is structurally unable to see. Funding for the operation traced back to Tornado Cash. No Balancer Labs or Aave statement is quoted in the reporting; Balancer holders have separately approved a protocol wind-down plan unrelated to this specific episode.
**Bug class:** balance-snapshot-monitoring-blind-spot — compliance/risk tooling that checks account balances at fixed intervals, rather than tracking intra-transaction fund flow, will not see assets that pass through and are returned before the next check.

### DarkSword iOS Exploit Framework — Enhanced "P7" Variant, Exposed C2 Infrastructure (Update)

Building on the prior-window advisory, researchers now describe an enhanced "P7 DarkSword" variant of the iOS exploit kit that adds two-way command-and-control communication and the ability to pull data from both the iOS Keychain and installed cryptocurrency wallet apps, with SlowMist warning the exploit chain may have been adapted to reach devices running iOS 26.5 in addition to the previously-flagged 18.6-18.7.2 range. Separately, researchers reported exposing parts of DarkSword's live C2 server infrastructure, offering a clearer picture of the campaign's backend without, per available reporting, disrupting it. No dollar-loss figure tying this framework to a specific crypto-theft total has been published.
**Bug class:** mobile-OS-exploit / wallet-keychain-theft.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Flash-loan-funded governance capture, now with instant financing (X100, new this window)**

Arkadiko showed vote-buying with spot capital; X100 shows the same failure mode financed by a flash loan — borrow, buy governance power, pass a proposal, drain, repay, all before the block ends. For any protocol with on-chain DAO control: check whether voting power is based on a live balance (flash-loan-attackable) or a historical snapshot, whether there's a minimum holding period before votes count, and whether a timelock separates proposal passage from execution. "No snapshot, no timelock" is now a two-incident pattern in two weeks, not a one-off.

**2. VM-level state forgery that bypasses balance/supply checks (Initia pattern, reinforced this window)**

Initia's own disclosure narrows the Day-2 mystery to something concrete: a script-caching layer in the VM let an attacker write a forged account state that credited a balance without updating the corresponding supply counter. For any chain or rollup with a custom VM, caching layer, or precompile: ask specifically whether cached/forged execution state can diverge from the canonical ledger's backing accounting, and whether bridges trust the former without re-deriving the latter independently.

**3. Hot keys that double as oracles (Swell, new this window)**

Swell's near-miss shows the blast radius when an "operational" hot key is also the key that sets a price (the exchange-rate oracle bot): compromise of one key both inflates a reported price AND retains the authority to stage fund-draining upgrades, two attack primitives from a single credential. When reviewing any protocol with an off-chain rate-setting bot, map whether that bot's signing key is isolated from upgrade/admin authority, and whether a sudden, maximum-rate-of-change price move (here, exactly 1%/hour, the system's own ceiling) triggers an automatic circuit breaker rather than relying on a human noticing.

**4. Balance snapshots vs. fund-flow tracking (Balancer V1/Aave, new this window)**

Blockaid's retrospective is a direct lesson for anyone building or relying on on-chain monitoring: a position that opens and closes within one transaction is invisible to anything that checks balances between blocks rather than tracing flow within them. If you're building detection tooling (or evaluating a protocol's reliance on third-party monitoring as a safety net), treat "no balance change at the snapshot boundary" as insufficient evidence that nothing happened.

**5. Incident-response credibility still lagging disclosure (standing, reinforced this window)**

Cysic's bounty offer went nowhere and the attacker laundered through Tornado Cash instead; WAX's first statement is self-reported and unverified; Initia's new root-cause disclosure, while a genuine improvement over last window's silence, still comes only from the team itself with no independent technical confirmation. Read the on-chain trail (Bitquery, PeckShield, Blockaid, Arkham), not just the project's own account, before treating any of these as closed.
