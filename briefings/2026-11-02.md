## 1. 🔴 Active exploits (last 24h)

### Premia Labs — Flash-Loan Empty-Options-Pool Drain on Arbitrum, ~$400,500-$464,600 (Disputed)

**Chain:** Arbitrum.
**Status:** Attack completed; funds already bridged and converted to BTC. No post-mortem or compensation plan published by Premia Labs as of writing.
**Impact:** Disputed between two independent, non-additive assessments. Blockaid's live alert — posted while the attack was still running, around 03:15 UTC on November 1 — estimated "~$400.5k drained from the vaults so far across ~2 txs." PeckShield's subsequent final tally put the total at **$464,600**, equivalent to roughly 131 ETH. crypto.news's reporting is explicit that "the different loss estimates reflect separate assessments... and should not be treated as additional thefts" — i.e., these are two estimates of the same event, not sequential losses. Six vaults were affected, holding a combined 3.64 WBTC, ~43.47 WETH, 48,479 USDC.e and 46,215 ARB.
**What happened:** The attacker created empty options pools on Premia's options-vault protocol, then used flash loans to purchase options in a way that caused vault collateral to become locked inside those otherwise-empty pools. Once locked, the attacker withdrew that collateral out as liquidity. Two principal withdrawal transactions occurred roughly one second apart around 03:15 UTC on November 1. Because flash loans let an attacker borrow without posting traditional collateral — provided the loan is repaid within the same transaction — failure cost the attacker only gas, letting the pool-creation/lock/drain sequence be attempted essentially for free until it worked.
**Root cause:** Not yet confirmed in a published post-mortem. The working characterization (Blockaid, PeckShield, crypto.news) is a logic flaw in how empty/newly-created options pools interact with vault collateral accounting — an attacker-controlled pool should not have been able to pull real vault collateral into itself via option purchases funded by flash-loaned capital.
**Remediation:** None published. "Premia Labs had not released a detailed public explanation of the affected vaults, recovery arrangements, or any planned reimbursement at the time of the initial security reports" (crypto.news).
**Fund movement:** Stolen assets moved from Arbitrum to Ethereum, then through Chainflip and NEAR Intents (both cross-chain swap services) into Bitcoin. The reporting is explicit that "the reported transfers do not establish that either platform knowingly assisted the attacker."
**Bug class:** logic-error / flash-loan-enabled empty-pool collateral lock-and-drain in an options-vault protocol.

### Name.com DNS Hijack — "Step Drainer" Served Across 12-15 DeFi Front-Ends, ~$138,000-$202,000 (Disputed, Already Resolved)

**Chain:** Front-end/DNS layer, affecting DeFi protocols across EVM chains, Solana, Aptos and Monad.
**Status:** Resolved. Name.com restored all affected domains; underlying smart contracts were not touched on any affected protocol.
**Impact:** Disputed between two independent trace efforts, tracking different wallet sets. The Block traced "about $138,000 in stolen funds... to three wallets on EVM chains, a figure that excludes any losses on Solana or Aptos," split in fixed 15%/55%/30% cuts among the three wallets, plus a separate ~3.1 BNB (~$2,200) to a Safe wallet from four BNB Chain wallets. Bitquery's independent investigation counted **~$202,000 drained from 32 crypto wallets across six chains** — a wider scope than The Block's EVM-only trace. Neither figure has been reconciled against the other; both should be treated as live estimates of overlapping but not identical scope.
**What happened:** Late on October 31, attackers compromised DNS records at domain registrar Name.com, affecting at least 12 domains (per Blockaid's initial count) and as many as 15 (per Name.com's own later count). Affected sites included Flap, Kintsu, Pickle Finance, Origami Finance, FluxBeam, Echelon, StonkFun, StakePoint, SupSwap, Longbow, SHPRD and ApeX DeFi — spanning EVM chains, Solana (FluxBeam, StonkFun, StakePoint), Aptos (Echelon) and Monad (Kintsu). The hijacked sites were rigged to serve **Step Drainer**, a malware-as-a-service wallet-drainer kit that LevelBlue SpiderLabs describes as sold for about $750 for full source code or $150 for a version that takes a 20% cut of proceeds, claiming support for more than 20 blockchain networks. The kit fakes normal-looking wallet-approval pop-ups to trick connected visitors into signing away funds.
**Root cause:** A single point of failure at the registrar level — all 12+ affected domains were registered through the same company, Name.com. Kintsu and Origami separately pointed to an "active attack on Vercel-associated Nameservers"; Vercel's own security bulletin confirmed 10 of the affected domains were tied to its platform and said it was "assisting in the investigation."
**Remediation:** Ten domains were placed into registrar-level "client hold" (which stops DNS resolution) by 8:32 a.m. ET on October 31. Name.com's statement: "name.com identified unauthorized activity late Friday... name.com has restored all affected domains and is working directly with the name.com account holders." Several affected protocols (FluxBeam, StonkFun) reported no known victims on their platforms specifically.
**Bug class:** supply-chain / domain-registrar DNS compromise enabling wallet-drainer-kit injection into otherwise-unmodified DeFi front-ends — contracts were never touched; this is purely a front-end/DNS-layer attack.

### Talis Protocol — NFT-Escrow Logic Exploit on Injective, ~5,700 NFTs Drained, Only ~$3,100 Realized

**Chain:** Injective (Talis also paused operations on other chains it supports, as a precaution).
**Status:** Active remediation in progress; listings/auctions halted platform-wide; post-mortem promised but not yet published.
**Impact:** Small in dollar terms despite the scale of assets touched: the attacker's net proceeds were "about 445 INJ, worth roughly $3,100 at the time" (CryptoBriefing), because most of the ~5,700 stolen NFTs were immediately dumped for around 2 cents each, mainly to automated bid bots, rather than sold at anything close to fair value.
**What happened:** On or around November 1, an attacker targeted vulnerabilities in two parts of Talis's platform — the marketplace contract and the auction contract — both of which held NFTs in escrow. After withdrawing the escrowed NFTs, the attacker sold them into pre-existing "open collection offers" (standing bids on any item from a given collection), letting the attacker unload the stolen assets instantly without waiting for a buyer, which is why realized proceeds were so low relative to the number of items taken.
**Root cause:** Not yet confirmed. CryptoBriefing's reporting is explicit that a forthcoming post-mortem "should show whether the flaw was a simple logic error or something more complex" in the escrow/marketplace-auction interaction — no specific bug class (reentrancy, access control, etc.) has been named by Talis as of writing.
**Remediation:** Talis halted all new listings and auctions platform-wide, paused operations on other supported chains as a precaution, has begun returning more than 26,000 NFTs held in escrow to their rightful owners (a broader recovery effort beyond just the stolen items), intends to compensate affected bidders, has a patched contract version under pre-deployment review, plans to publish a list of affected NFTs, and warned users against buying any of the stolen items.
**Bug class:** access-control / NFT-escrow-logic flaw in marketplace and auction contracts, compounded by a drain-then-dump-via-standing-collection-offers mechanism that let the attacker liquidate near-instantly at the cost of realizing almost none of the stolen value.

### Binance — $6.3-6.4B Hack: FBI's First Official Attribution, Withdrawals Resume in Phases, Laundering Continues (Day 4)

**Chain:** Multi-chain (Ethereum, Bitcoin, Tron, XRP Ledger, Solana, Arbitrum, Optimism, Base, Avalanche, Polygon per TRM Labs' trace); this window's laundering activity concentrated on Tron and cross-chain swap services (Relay, THORChain, Chainflip, Symbiosis, Bitget Wallet).
**Status:** Active remediation, now in a withdrawal-restoration phase. Withdrawals resumed in stages starting November 1; investigation into "additional points of breach" beyond the previously-reported compromised DevOps laptop remains open per Binance's own prior statement, with no new root-cause detail published this window.
**Impact:** The total theft figure remains stable at the converged $6.3-6.4B range reported previously (TRM Labs ~$6.36B, Chainalysis $6.4B, Binance's own ~$6.3B). What moved this window is coverage and recovery, not the theft total: Binance says it has now replaced **~95% of affected assets** using company funds — including its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU), corporate treasury, and futures insurance fund — up from 90% a day earlier (CoinDesk/CNBC). Binance states: "All BTC, ETH, USDT, BNB, SOL, USDC, XRP, USD1, RLUSD, and ZEC balances are fully backed 100%," pointing users to its proof-of-reserves snapshot, with a fuller audit-style PoR report still promised. Separately, roughly **$3 billion** remains frozen/immobilized, mostly in stablecoins whose issuers can block transfers — unchanged in scale from prior reporting (Tether ~$1.567B+, Circle ~$800M, plus Ripple and others).
**What happened (withdrawal restart):** Binance's own statement: "Binance is preparing to resume phase one of withdrawals on 2026-11-01 between 06:00 and 09:00 (UTC)." Bitcoin led the restart, with ether, USDT, USDC, BNB and XRP — "the most requested networks" — reopened alongside it the same day, plus assets on 15 other blockchains. Further phases remain gated on each blockchain clearing additional security and stability checks. A separate report (CryptoBriefing) tracked roughly $3.8 billion withdrawn in the first 10 hours after reopening.
**Attribution — now officially confirmed by the US government:** This is the most significant development of the window. Previously, North Korea/TraderTraitor attribution rested solely on private-sector analysis (Chainalysis, TRM Labs, ZachXBT). On October 31, the **FBI issued a formal alert** — jointly with the Secret Service and IRS Criminal Investigation — that, per CryptoSlate's direct citation, "attributed the Oct. 29 attack to North Korean cyber actors," which CryptoSlate characterizes as "official US government confirmation of the perpetrators." The alert states investigators "observed the attackers converting stolen assets primarily into Ethereum" and warns the funds would move through DeFi and instant-swap services. The FBI "published hundreds of cryptocurrency addresses associated with the theft and urged THORNode operators, RPC node operators, DeFi platforms, instant exchanges and other service providers to block transactions involving the identified wallets." (This series could not independently fetch the FBI's own alert page at fbi.gov, which returned HTTP 403 to automated requests; the attribution and request language above is as quoted by CryptoSlate and corroborated by The Block's separate reporting on the same alert.) THORChain co-founder Chad Barraford publicly pushed back the same evening, posting that "THORNode operators have zero information that isn't already public and readily available in real time," adding "No subpoenas required, a best case scenario for investigations" — without committing to actually block the listed addresses.
**Laundering trail (new detail this window):** On-chain analyst SomaXBT, a Security Alliance (SEAL) contributor, tracked roughly **$242 million** laundered via Tron within two days, out of about $628 million that left Binance via Tron in the original theft. Of that, ~$194 million left Tron entirely via bridges/swaps (Relay ~$48M, THORChain ~$44M, Chainflip ~$32.1M, Symbiosis ~$16M, with a further ~$41.7M routed to Bitget Wallet whose ultimate destination is unresolved); ~$26 million remains on Tron; ~$22 million was lost to fees/slippage/price moves; and Tether's freeze of 378.7 million USDT mid-stream left roughly $249 million still unmoved. Separately, Lookonchain tracked the attacker converting **970,522 SOL (~$95.9 million)** into Bitcoin via Relay, including a ~486,970 SOL (~$46.4 million) tranche moved within a single hour.
**Root cause:** Unchanged from prior reporting — still described as a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop reaching a withdrawal-processing service container, plus unspecified "additional points of breach," with Binance maintaining there is no evidence of a leaked wallet private key. No new detail on this published this window.
**Bug class:** key-management / endpoint-and-signing-infrastructure compromise, unchanged; the EIP-7702 delegation mechanism remains the execution primitive, not the root vulnerability.

## 4. 🎯 What to hunt

**1. Empty/newly-created pools as a free attack surface when flash loans are in scope (Premia Labs, new this window)**

Premia's attacker created brand-new, empty options pools specifically to exploit how vault collateral gets locked in when options are purchased against a pool — then drained that collateral once locked. Any protocol letting users or counterparties create new pools/markets on demand, combined with flash-loan-reachable capital, should get a specific pass asking: can an attacker-created, zero-liquidity pool/market be used as a destination to pull real collateral out of an existing, legitimate vault? Because flash loans make failed attempts free (only gas is lost), this class of bug tends to get probed repeatedly in production rather than caught in review — a reason to treat "pool creation" and "collateral accounting across pools" as a single trust boundary during audit, not two separate concerns.

**2. Registrar-level DNS compromise as a single point of failure across unrelated protocols (Name.com/Step Drainer, new this window)**

Twelve-plus otherwise-unrelated DeFi front-ends (spanning EVM, Solana, Aptos, and Monad) went down simultaneously because they happened to share one domain registrar. The underlying smart contracts were never touched — this was purely a front-end/DNS-layer compromise, but it was fully sufficient to drain connected wallets via a commodity drainer-as-a-service kit costing as little as $150. For bounty/audit purposes, this is a reminder that front-end security posture (registrar 2FA/registry-lock, DNSSEC, CAA records, nameserver diversity) is in scope for any program claiming to cover "the application," not just the on-chain contracts — and that a single compromised registrar account can be a blast-radius multiplier across many otherwise-unconnected protocols that happen to share it.

**3. Escrow-plus-standing-offer designs can let an attacker monetize stolen assets before anyone can react (Talis Protocol, new this window)**

Talis's attacker drained thousands of escrowed NFTs but only realized about $3,100, because the exploit path let them dump stolen items directly into pre-existing standing "collection offer" bids rather than needing to find buyers — trading value for speed. Any marketplace or vault design that lets a holder of illegitimately-obtained assets immediately liquidate into a standing bid/offer book (rather than requiring a matched counterparty or a withdrawal delay) is worth checking specifically for the "drain then instantly dump into our own liquidity" pattern, since it removes the usual window defenders rely on to freeze or blacklist stolen funds before they're monetized.

**4. Official government attribution changes incident-response obligations, not the underlying bug (Binance, updated this window)**

The FBI's alert and address list are now the first case in this series where private-sector attribution (already strong) has been converted into an official government confirmation with a specific compliance ask attached (block these addresses). For teams running DeFi infrastructure that touches cross-chain swaps, instant exchanges, or liquidity routing — especially anything resembling THORChain's permissionless, no-KYC node model — this is a live test of whether "we have no special information beyond what's public on-chain" (as THORChain's own co-founder argued) is a sufficient response once a government agency has published a named address list. Worth reviewing whether your own project's incident playbook has a defined process for responding to a law-enforcement blocklist request, independent of whether you agree node operators have an obligation to act on it.
